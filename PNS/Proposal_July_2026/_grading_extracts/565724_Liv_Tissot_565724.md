[0017] [Heading 4] Abstract

[0018] [LO-normal] Regular meal timing is one of the factors which determine our wellbeing. Our body enjoys routine, and consistent eating schedules help us set our circadian rhythm. This proposed study considers whether regular meal times will influence our emotional wellbeing, and how sex differences affect this.

[0019] [LO-normal] Using an experimental between-subjects design, 256 participants will be recruited from social media platforms to take part in a 14 day experiment. The participants will be randomly assigned to the control or test groups, where the test group is given allotted meal times. Participants will record their meal times and fill out the WHO-5 Wellbeing Index each day, and this data will be analyzed in IBM SPSS Statistics, using a two-way independent ANOVA test.

[0020] [LO-normal] It is hypothesized that participants in the test group will score higher on the WHO-5 , and that women will show a significantly higher difference between the test and control groups. This proposed study will add to the pool of existing literature surrounding diet and wellbeing, and provide valuable insight on the specific role of routine eating habits on emotional wellbeing.

[0023] [Heading 4] Introduction

[0024] [Heading 5] Literature review

[0025] [LO-normal] Eating habits, including deviant eating behaviour such as regularly skipping meals or overeating has a significant association with mental wellbeing (Morshed et al, 2022). The extent of this wellbeing is prompted by several factors, one is the motivation associated with the dietary patterns we follow (Nestorowicz et al, 2020). These habits which encompass the consistent frequency of our meals are tied to our overall brain function, including; slowing cognitive decline, stronger neurodevelopment, and emotional wellbeing (Granero & Guillazo-Blanch, 2025).

[0026] [LO-normal] The gut-brain axis connects the gastrointestinal (GI) system and the central nervous system (CNS), this connection greatly impacts emotional wellbeing (Patil et al, 2025). Alongside our Circadian Rhythm the GI forms a loop which influences our gut microbes, and helps us in maintaining our sleeping cycles. Our eating habits also serve to regulate our emotions through lowering stress hormones such as cortisol. The creation and maintenance of one’s gut microbiome affects our emotional and neurological health, making healthy eating patterns important to maintaining ourselves. One of the factors which influences this is regular meal times (Kim et al, 2025).

[0028] [LO-normal] The Circadian Rhythm is known to have sex differences. According to Walton et al (2022), some of these differences include physiological responses such as body temperature, heart rate, and in the Hypothalamic Pituitary Gonadal (HPG) Axis and Hypothalamic Pituitary Adrenal (HPA) Axis. Lok et al (2024) support this by highlighting that the differences in Circadian Rhythm also modulate metabolic control, which affects nutrition, energy production, and energy balance.

[0029] [LO-normal] Moreover, Diez-hernandez et al (2026) explain that sex has a significant effect on dietary habits, and the resulting effect on wellbeing. With women overall exuding a higher prevalence of deviant eating patterns, as well as psychological issues related to food. These findings highlight the importance of considering not only the quality of the diet but also its consistency.

[0031] [Heading 5] Rationale

[0032] [LO-normal] The proposed study aims to support the literature on the effect of lifestyle on wellbeing. We expect predicted findings to support the existing literature on the differing dietary effects between men and women (Imamura et al, 2015: Mauro et al, 2020), as well as the effect of diet regularity on wellbeing (Appleton, 2018: Gershon & Margolis, 2021).

[0033] [LO-normal] There is a research gap in the effect of diet regularity on emotional wellbeing as the only independent variable (IV). Some studies, such as Bedache et al (2021) or Jimenez-Morcillo and Clemente-Suarez (2023) investigate the different relationships sexes have towards healthy living and wellbeing, however, these studies consider the impact of several different variables including strength-training and lifestyle. This leads to our research question: “Is greater consistency of meal timing associated with emotional wellbeing in young adults, and is this relationship moderated by biological sex?”

[0034] [LO-normal] Hypothesis: “Participants in the test group with regular meal times will score significantly higher than the control group on the WHO-5 wellbeing index.”

[0035] [LO-normal] Hypothesis: “Women will demonstrate a significantly higher difference between the test and control groups compared to men.”

[0036] [LO-normal] Null hypothesis: “There will not be a significant difference between groups.”

[0038] [Heading 4] Research design

[0039] [Heading 5] Design

[0040] [LO-normal] This study is using an experimental between-subjects design. Participants will be divided by sex before being randomly assigned to the test or control groups which will be compared to see whether the independent variable (IV) has a significant impact on the dependent variable (DV). The control group will have standardised meal times, while the test group will not. Meal timing variance will be recorded alongside the WHO-5 wellbeing index and will be completed each day.

[0042] [Heading 5] Materials

[0043] [LO-normal] The apparatus needed for this study includes the WHO-5 wellbeing index, as seen in Appendix A, Qualtrics, and SPSS. The WHO-5 wellbeing index is a 5 question index measured on a scale from 0-5, where 0 indicates low wellbeing and 5 indicates high wellbeing. The scoring of this index ranges from 0 to 25, this score is then multiplied by 4 to have a percentage out of 100%. A score below 50 is considered an indicator for poor mental health (WHO, 2024).

[0044] [LO-normal] The platform Qualtrics will be used to record the data. It is an online hub for academic surveys which will allow participants to answer the WHO-5 and record their meal times each day, which can be seen in Appendix B. SPSS will be used for data analysis, as it is considered to be the ideal tool for this within social sciences (Rahman & Muktadir, 2021).

[0045] [LO-normal] Finally, a consent form, as shown in Appendix C will be provided during the recruitment phase. This form will explain what will be expected of participants and give them the chance to opt out of the study. It will include a debriefing and explain the right to withdraw as well as how their data will be kept anonymous.

[0047] [Heading 5] Participants

[0048] [LO-normal] In order to achieve standard statistical power we will use quota sampling to recruit 256 participants, with 64 in each group. With this we can test for a medium effect size. Participant recruitment will take place through social media as this is an efficient and cost effective strategy when recruiting young adults (Darko et al, 2022). Facebook and Instagram have proven to be efficient in finding specific populations to recruit (Smith et al, 2023) and will be employed for this process.

[0049] [LO-normal] Participants will be within the traditional young adult ages of 20 to 35, and they must have no history of insomnia or any health concerns regarding dietary choices, such as diabetes, nor irregular sleep schedules, as these can disrupt the circadian rhythm (Gentry et al, 2021).

Once this is completed, we can begin our data analysis.

This test will show us three things, which are: the main effect of each IV, and the interaction effect. To prepare for the test, we create three columns, one with sex, one with condition, and one with test scores. Both latter columns must be coded with labels.

[0074] [Heading 5] Procedure

[0075] [LO-normal] To begin, a Qualtrics survey will be created, and the contents of the WHO-5 wellbeing Index, will be added to it, as well as an open answer question to record meal times.

[0076] [LO-normal] Following this, we must recruit participants. Using facebook and instagram a total of 256 participants will be recruited and assigned to the control or test groups using stratified randomization, to have an equal number of men and women in each group.

[0077] [LO-normal] Informed consent will be gathered through a consent form, which will include a debriefing about the experiment, as well as informing the participants about the right to withdraw and giving them access to the researcher’s contact information concerning any further questions they may have.

[0078] [LO-normal] Once the participant quota has been met, we can begin the experiment. Over a 14 day period, the participants will record their meal times, as well as their WHO-5 wellbeing index score and record these in a qualtrics survey. Once the collection period ends, the data analysis takes place in SPSS using a two-way independent ANOVA test, this allows us to search for a significant relationship between the IV’s and the DV.

[0080] [Heading 5] Ethics

[0081] [LO-normal] In order to conduct this study we must meet the ethical standards. This includes gaining informed consent, clarifying the right to withdraw, and ensuring data anonymity. In order to achieve this, an online consent and debriefing form will be provided, which will require a signature before the test can begin. A debriefing will be written out for the participants, ensuring they are aware of the purpose of the study and their right to withdraw.

[0082] [LO-normal] Due to our design requiring the control groups to have assignment meal times, we must be able to ethically justify this. The British Psychological Society (BPS) requires risk assessment. For this study, meal timing is an essential IV which is necessary to conduct the experiment. In order to mitigate harm, the instructed meal times will fall within the bounds of normal life, without causing metabolic drops. Populations with health concerns will be excluded from the experiment. This will be explicitly made clear within the consent form in order for the participants to understand what is expected of them and whether they feel they are suited to the study.

[0083] [LO-normal] Ethical approval will be gained from the University of Essex Online, Department of Psychology, ensuring that the research will adhere to The BPS (BPS, 2021) standards.

[0085] [Heading 5] Limitations

[0086] [LO-normal] There are several limitations to the proposed study. First, using a between subjects design has some constraints, including the need for a large sample size to achieve statistical power. Furthermore, individual differences between participants, such as age, mood, or metabolism can be a confounding variable on the results. We mitigate this by randomly assigning participants to a condition.

[0087] [LO-normal] Additionally the participants are at risk of a self-report bias, wherein their answers are not entirely accurate to what is real. Moreso, there are confounding variables such as life stressors outside of the experiment, which could affect the answers given on the wellbeing scale.
