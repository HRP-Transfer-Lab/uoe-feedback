[0023] [Heading 1] Introduction

[0024] [Heading 2] Background Literature Review

[0025] [Heading 3] Theory

[0026] [Normal] Staying in control of attention and behaviour under pressure relies on coordination between prefrontal regulatory regions and the autonomic nervous system. According to the neurovisceral integration model, vagally mediated heart rate variability (HRV) reflects how well a broader central autonomic network supports flexible self-regulation (Thayer & Lane, 2000, 2009). Resting HRV is therefore a peripheral marker of regulatory capacity rather than a direct measure of neural activity, so people with higher resting HRV should find it easier to stay flexible and goal-directed as cognitive demands rise.

[0027] [Heading 3] Prior Findings

[0028] [Normal] On the whole, the evidence supports a link between resting HRV and cognition. Reviews and recent empirical studies connect higher resting HRV with stronger executive functioning, including working memory and inhibitory control, in healthy adults (Arakaki et al., 2023; Forte & Casagrande, 2025). Whether this reflects a straightforward causal relationship is less certain: Nashiro et al. (2023) found no overall improvement in inhibitory control after a five-week HRV-biofeedback intervention, although participants who showed greater heart-rate oscillation during training did improve on the Flanker task. This points to resting HRV working better as a marker of individual differences than as something that straightforwardly causes better cognitive performance.

[0029] [Normal] What remains unclear is whether the HRV–cognition relationship shifts as cognitive demand increases. Riganello et al. (2023) examined autonomic indices during inhibition tasks of varying complexity, but did not test whether resting HRV predicts inhibitory control more strongly once load is manipulated. Doneva and De Fockert (2014) showed that a concurrent working-memory demand on the Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART) pushed participants towards a more conservative response criterion. Together, these findings point to cognitive demand as a possible boundary condition on the HRV–inhibitory-control relationship.

[0030] [Normal] Neurovisceral integration theory holds that higher resting HRV reflects a greater capacity for flexible prefrontal-autonomic regulation. This capacity may matter little when regulatory demands are low, but more once executive resources are stretched by a concurrent working-memory load, so cognitive load may reveal differences that stay hidden under easier conditions.

[0031] [Heading 2] Rationale and Hypotheses

[0032] [Normal] This proposal goes beyond simply asking whether HRV and executive performance are correlated. Its originality lies in combining a standardised resting physiological measure with an experimentally manipulated, within-person cognitive-load factor and testing how the two interact, giving a more specific test of when an HRV–performance relationship should show up most clearly, without claiming that higher resting HRV itself causes better cognitive functioning.

[0033] [Normal] The research question is: In healthy working-age adults, is resting HRV associated with performance on a Go/No-Go inhibitory-control task, and does this association become stronger under high relative to low cognitive load?

[0034] [Normal] H1: Higher resting HRV will be associated with better inhibitory-control performance, indexed by greater d′.

[0035] [Normal] H2: The positive association between resting HRV and inhibitory-control performance will be stronger under high cognitive load than under low cognitive load, demonstrated by a significant HRV × cognitive-load interaction.

[0036] [Normal] The corresponding null hypotheses are that resting HRV will not be significantly associated with inhibitory-control performance, and that there will be no significant HRV × cognitive-load interaction.

[0037] [Heading 2] Aims and Objectives

[0038] [Normal] My primary aim is to determine whether resting HRV predicts inhibitory-control performance more strongly under high than low cognitive load in healthy working-age adults. To get there, I plan to: (1) obtain a standardised measure of resting HRV; (2) measure response inhibition and sustained attentional control under low and high concurrent working-memory load; and (3) test whether cognitive load moderates the relationship between resting HRV and task performance.

[0039] [Heading 1] Research Design

[0040] [Heading 2] Design

[0041] [Normal] This proposal takes a post-positivist, quantitative approach, treating resting HRV and behavioural performance as measurable, if imperfect, indicators of the underlying regulatory processes described by neurovisceral integration theory.

[0042] [Normal] I will use a mixed experimental-correlational repeated-measures design. Resting HRV is a measured, continuous, between-person predictor, while cognitive load is manipulated within participants at two levels, low and high, with order counterbalanced. The primary behavioural outcome is d′, from SART performance.

[0043] [Normal] Because HRV is measured rather than manipulated, this design cannot show that HRV causes differences in inhibitory-control performance. The within-participant manipulation of load, though, can support a causal claim about its effect on performance, provided the manipulation works as intended and order effects are controlled.

[0044] [Heading 2] Materials

[0045] [Normal] I will assess inhibitory control using the SART (Robertson et al., 1997), where participants respond to frequent digit stimuli but withhold responding to an infrequent target. SART performance depends on both response inhibition and sustained attention, so I will not treat it as a process-pure measure of inhibitory control.

[0046] [Normal] Cognitive load will be manipulated with a concurrent digit-memory task, following Lavie et al. (2004): a one-digit set for low load and a six-digit set for high load. Participants will encode the set, hold it in mind while completing the SART, and then indicate whether a probe digit had appeared. Embedding this task alongside the SART in both conditions, following Doneva and De Fockert (2014), means the conditions differ in memory demand rather than simply in whether a secondary task is present. Accuracy on the digit-memory task will serve as a manipulation check.

[0047] [Normal] I will use signal-detection indices because they separate sensitivity from response strategy: d′ indexes how well a participant discriminates go from no-go trials, while criterion c indexes whether responding leans liberal or conservative. This matters here since Doneva and De Fockert (2014) found that working-memory load shifted response criterion rather than causing a blanket decline in performance.

[0048] [Normal] Resting HRV will be measured with research-grade electrocardiography (ECG), following Laborde et al. (2017). Participants will sit for a five-minute acclimatisation period followed by a five-minute baseline recording, in a standardised posture (feet flat, hands on thighs, minimal movement, spontaneous breathing). ECG will be sampled at a rate sufficient for reliable R-peak identification and checked for artefacts. The primary HRV index will be RMSSD (root mean square of successive differences).

[0049] [Normal] To limit transient influences on HRV, participants will be asked to keep their usual sleep routine, avoid intense physical training the day before testing, avoid alcohol for 24 hours, and avoid food and caffeinated drinks for two hours beforehand (Laborde et al., 2017). I will record sleep timing, smoking status, medication, and adherence, and schedule sessions at a similar time of day where feasible.

[0050] [Heading 2] Participants

[0051] [Normal] I will recruit a minimum of 80 adults aged 18–65 using convenience and snowball sampling. Participants will need normal or corrected-to-normal vision and enough English to follow the instructions. I will exclude anyone with a diagnosed cardiovascular or neurological condition, a current major psychiatric disorder, or medication known to substantially affect autonomic cardiac functioning.

[0052] [Normal] Age will be entered as a prespecified covariate, since it relates to HRV and could also affect cognitive performance.

[0053] [Normal] Because the primary hypothesis concerns an interaction effect, which generally needs more power than a main effect alone (Blake & Gangestad, 2020), sample size deserves care here. Comparable HRV-cognition studies have used samples from around 36 (Riganello et al., 2023) to 143 (Forte & Casagrande, 2025). I propose 80 as a minimum feasibility benchmark, not proof of adequate power. Before a full dissertation study, I would settle on a final sample size through an a priori or simulation-based power analysis targeting the HRV × cognitive-load interaction.

[0054] [Heading 1] Data Analysis Plan

[0055] [Normal] I will first inspect ECG recordings for ectopic beats and artefacts before calculating RMSSD, applying a natural-log transformation if RMSSD shows substantial positive skew, in line with recommendations for HRV analysis (Laborde et al., 2017).

[0056] [Normal] For the signal-detection calculations, I will classify correct responses to go trials as hits and responses to no-go targets as false alarms, then calculate d′ and criterion c separately for each load condition. Extreme hit or false-alarm rates of 0 or 1 will be corrected using the log-linear adjustment described by Hautus (1995).

[0057] [Normal] The primary analysis will use a linear mixed-effects model, with cognitive load effect-coded (low = −0.5; high = +0.5) and resting HRV mean-centred. Fixed effects will include HRV, load, the HRV × load interaction, and age, with participant as a random intercept and a random slope for load retained where the model converges.

[0058] [Normal] H1 will be tested against the fixed effect of resting HRV, and H2 against the HRV × cognitive-load interaction, evaluating significance at α = .05 and reporting 95% confidence intervals, with model assumptions checked through residual diagnostics.

[0059] [Normal] d′ is the primary outcome. Criterion c will be analysed as a secondary outcome using the same model, since Doneva and De Fockert’s (2014) findings centred on response criterion, and reaction time will be retained as a secondary descriptive measure, since it alone cannot distinguish improved inhibition from a speed-accuracy trade-off. Memory-task accuracy will be compared across load conditions to check the manipulation worked as intended.

[0060] [Heading 1] Procedure

[0061] [Normal] Participants will first receive the Participant Information Sheet (Appendix A) and give written informed consent via the Consent Form (Appendix B), before completing demographic and screening questions.

[0062] [Normal] They will then sit through a five-minute acclimatisation period followed by the five-minute resting ECG recording, receive standardised instructions and practice trials, and complete the SART under both load conditions, with order counterbalanced. At the end, participants will be debriefed and given a chance to ask questions. The whole session should take around 40–45 minutes.

[0068] [Heading 1] Limitations

[0069] [Normal] Because resting HRV is measured rather than manipulated, an association between HRV and inhibitory-control performance cannot establish causality. Even though cognitive load is manipulated, the HRV × load interaction would only show that this association varies with load, not that HRV itself causally produces resilience to cognitive demand. The neurovisceral integration model itself is also not beyond challenge: Grossman and Taylor (2007) argue that RSA-based HRV measures do not cleanly index a single, unitary vagal tone construct, since respiration and other non-neural factors can confound the signal, which means the model's central assumption, that resting HRV directly reflects prefrontal-autonomic regulatory capacity, is contested rather than settled.

[0070] [Normal] The SART is also not a process-pure measure of inhibition, since performance depends partly on sustained attention, so differences in d′ could reflect overlapping attentional processes rather than inhibition alone. Resting HRV is, in turn, influenced by sleep, recent activity, substance use, medication, and time of day; standardised procedures and screening will reduce these influences but not remove all residual variation.

[0071] [Normal] The proposed sample size is a feasibility estimate rather than the result of a formal power analysis, so the interaction may still be difficult to detect, and convenience and snowball sampling may limit how representative the sample is. This recruitment approach also risks producing a WEIRD sample, that is, one drawn from Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, and Democratic populations (Henrich et al., 2010), since people who respond to this kind of advert tend to be more educated and more digitally connected than the wider population. Recruiting through workplace occupational-health schemes and community organisations, rather than relying solely on personal and academic networks, would help reach a broader range of adults and reduce this bias in future iterations of the study.

[0072] [Normal] Finally, although demanding occupational contexts partly motivate this research, laboratory SART performance cannot demonstrate superior professional judgement or quality of care. Findings would be limited to the relationship between resting HRV and cognitive-control performance, and any real-world professional implications would need separate, ecologically valid investigation.
