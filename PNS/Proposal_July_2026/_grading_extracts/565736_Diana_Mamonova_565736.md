To what extent does sympathetic physiological reactivity predict attentional bias 
towards threatening stimuli in adults with varying levels of trait anxiety? 
Background Literature Review  
Anxiety involves both cognitive and physiological processes. Cognitive theories suggest 
that people with anxiety pay more attention to threatening information than neutral 
information, which may increase feelings of danger and help maintain anxiety 
symptoms (Beck & Clark, 1997). This tendency is known as attentional bias towards 
threat. It refers to a greater focus on threatening stimuli, diƯiculty shifting attention 
away from them, or avoiding them altogether (Cisler & Koster, 2010). Research has 
consistently found that anxious individuals show greater attentional bias towards threat 
than non-anxious individuals, although ﬁndings vary across diƯerent tasks and studies 
(Bar-Haim et al., 2007). 
Anxiety is also associated with changes in the body's physiological response to threat. 
The autonomic nervous system helps regulate these responses, particularly through 
activation of the sympathetic nervous system. One way to measure sympathetic activity 
is through skin conductance response (SCR), which reﬂects changes in sweat gland 
activity when people encounter emotionally signiﬁcant or threatening stimuli (Boucsein, 
2012; Dawson et al., 2017). SCR is widely used in psychophysiological research as a 
non-invasive measure of physiological reactivity. 
The present proposal uses the term sympathetic physiological reactivity rather than the 
broader construct physiological arousal. This distinction addresses the fact that SCR 
and heart-rate variability are not interchangeable. Whereas SCR is directly driven by 
sympathetic sudomotor activity, commonly used short-term heart-rate variability 
indices are strongly inﬂuenced by parasympathetic regulation. HRV 
is consequently excluded to maintain a single, theoretically coherent predictor.  
From a cognitive neuroscience perspective, threat processing involves several brain 
regions. The amygdala plays an important role in detecting threats, while areas of the 
prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex are involved in attention and emotional 
regulation (Bishop, 2007). These systems help explain why physiological responses and 
attentional processes may be closely related. Although the present study will not 
directly measure brain activity, it aims to examine whether sympathetic physiological 
responses to threat are associated with attentional bias towards threatening 
information. 
Prior Findings  
The dot-probe task is widely used to operationalise threat-related attention. A threat-
neutral stimulus pair is displayed, followed by a probe in the location of either stimulus. 
Faster responses on threat-congruent than threat-incongruent trials are interpreted as 


## Page 4
relatively greater allocation of attention towards threat (MacLeod et al., 1986). The 
conventional bias score is calculated as mean reaction time on threat-incongruent 
trials minus mean reaction time on threat-congruent trials, so a positive 
value indicates bias towards threat.  
However, studies examining attentional bias towards threat have produced inconsistent 
ﬁndings. Trial-level research suggests that vigilance, avoidance and disengagement 
patterns can be obscured when performance is reduced to one mean score (Evans et 
al., 2020). Recent work has also questioned whether trait anxiety consistently predicts 
dot-probe bias and has highlighted reliability limitations (Williams et al., 2024). These 
concerns do not make the paradigm invalid, but require careful task design, adequate 
trial numbers, transparent scoring and cautious interpretation.  
Research has separately associated anxiety with altered threat attention and autonomic 
responding, but fewer studies have directly tested whether stimulus-evoked 
sympathetic reactivity predicts attentional bias and whether this association varies 
across trait anxiety. Measuring trait anxiety continuously retains individual-diƯerence 
variability that would be lost by recruiting only high scorers. It also permits a moderation 
analysis rather than treating anxiety solely as an eligibility category.  
Theory  
The proposal is informed by cognitive models in which threat-related schemas 
increase prioritisation of danger cues (Beck & Clark, 1997), and by Attentional Control 
Theory, which argues that anxiety disrupts goal-directed control and increases the 
inﬂuence of stimulus-driven processing (Eysenck et al., 2007). Bringing these ideas 
together suggests the following mechanism: people who are more sensitive to threat 
tend to show a stronger sympathetic nervous system response when they encounter 
threat, and this stronger response is linked to greater attention towards threatening 
information. Trait anxiety may strengthen this link because people with higher trait 
anxiety are more likely to interpret ambiguous or negative cues as threatening. However, 
this is a theoretical and statistical prediction, not a claim that skin conductance 
response (SCR) directly causes attentional bias. 
 
Rationale and Hypotheses  
The study addresses the question: To what extent does sympathetic physiological 
reactivity, measured by SCR, predict attentional bias towards threatening stimuli, and is 
this relationship moderated by trait anxiety in adults? This study is important because it 
examines physiological and cognitive processes together rather than separately.

H1: Greater threat-speciﬁc SCR will signiﬁcantly predict a more positive dot-probe 
attentional-bias score.  
H2: Trait anxiety will moderate the relationship between threat-speciﬁc SCR and 
attentional bias, such that the positive association will be stronger at higher trait-anxiety 
levels.  
The corresponding null hypotheses are that threat-speciﬁc SCR will not predict 
attentional-bias scores and that the SCR × trait-anxiety interaction will not explain 
signiﬁcant additional variance.  
Aims and Objectives  
The primary aim is to determine whether sympathetic reactivity to threat predicts 
attentional bias and whether trait anxiety moderates that association. The objectives 
are to: (1) quantify threat-speciﬁc SCR; (2) produce a valid reaction-time attentional-
bias score from a facial dot-probe task; (3) measure trait anxiety continuously using the 
trait scale of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory; and (4) test the predictor, moderator and 
interaction within a prespeciﬁed regression model.

Research Design  
Design  
A quantitative, cross-sectional, laboratory-based correlational design will be used. The 
predictor will be threat-speciﬁc SCR, operationalised as the mean event-related SCR 
amplitude for threatening-face trials minus the mean amplitude for neutral control 
trials. The outcome will be the dot-probe attentional-bias score. Trait anxiety will be the 
continuous moderator. Because variables are measured rather than manipulated, the 
design can establish statistical prediction and association but not causation.  
Materials  
Electrodermal activity will be recorded using calibrated skin-conductance equipment 
with disposable electrodes placed on the palmar surface of the non-dominant hand. A 
brief resting baseline will precede the task. Event markers will synchronise stimulus 
onset and physiological recording. SCR scoring windows, minimum 
response threshold and treatment of non-responses will be speciﬁed before data 
collection and applied consistently in accordance with electrodermal measurement 
guidance (Boucsein, 2012).  
The computerised dot-probe task will present validated angry and neutral facial 
expressions from the same actors, counterbalanced across screen position and probe 
congruency. Each trial will include ﬁxation, a face pair, and a probe requiring a left or 
right key response. Accuracy and reaction time will be recorded. Incorrect responses, 
anticipatory responses and implausibly slow responses will be removed using 
prespeciﬁed criteria. The trait subscale of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory will 
measure relatively stable anxiety proneness (Spielberger et al., 1983). A demographic 
and health questionnaire (to follow), will record age, corrected vision, relevant 
medication, caƯeine, nicotine, recent exercise and sleep because these may aƯect 
autonomic measurement.  
Participants  
Approximately 80 adults aged 18–65 will be recruited from the university participant 
pool and community advertisements. The ﬁnal sample size should be conﬁrmed using a 
power analysis carried out before the study. This analysis should be based on an 
expected eƯect size from previous research, with a signiﬁcance level of .05 and 
statistical power of .80. 
Eligible participants must be able to provide informed consent, understand 
written English and have normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Exclusion criteria will 
include a self-reported neurological condition, current psychosis, signiﬁcant 
uncorrected visual impairment, or medication known to substantially aƯect autonomic 
functioning. Exclusions will be applied cautiously and transparently, and participants

will not be asked to stop prescribed medication. Participants will be informed that they 
have the right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty and without 
providing a reason. Recruitment across the full trait-anxiety range avoids range 
restriction and supports the moderation question. 
 
Data Analysis Plan  
Data will be analysed in IBM SPSS Statistics or equivalent software. Preparation will 
include accuracy checks, missing-data assessment, preregistered reaction-time 
exclusions, inspection of SCR artefacts and calculation of threat-speciﬁc SCR and 
attentional-bias scores. Internal consistency of the STAI trait scale will be reported. 
Descriptive statistics and zero-order correlations will summarise the variables.  
The primary analysis will be hierarchical multiple regression. Mean-centred threat-
speciﬁc SCR and trait anxiety will be entered as main eƯects, followed by their product 
term. H1 will be evaluated from the SCR regression coeƯicient. H2 will be evaluated 
from the interaction coeƯicient and change in explained variance after adding the 
interaction. A signiﬁcant interaction will be probed using simple slopes at 
low, mean and high trait-anxiety values, with conﬁdence intervals reported. Regression 
assumptions will be assessed through residual plots, normality of residuals, 
homoscedasticity, linearity, inﬂuence statistics and variance-inﬂation factors. If 
assumptions are violated, additional analyses will be considered to check the 
robustness of ﬁndings. 
This analysis is suitable because the study examines whether a continuous 
physiological measure predicts a continuous behavioural outcome, and whether this 
relationship changes depending on another continuous variable. Statistical signiﬁcance 
will be assessed at p < .05, but greater emphasis will be placed on eƯect sizes and 95% 
conﬁdence intervals. The third hypothesis from the earlier outline, which suggested 
splitting participants into high- and low-anxiety groups, was removed because dividing 
STAI scores into categories would reduce the amount of information available and does 
not ﬁt with the continuous moderation approach. 
Procedure  
After ethics approval, prospective participants will receive an information sheet and 
provide informed consent. They will be reminded that participation is voluntary and that 
withdrawal is possible without disadvantage. Participants will complete the 
demographic and health questions and STAI trait scale. The electrode sites will be 
cleaned, sensors attached and participants will be explained that the equipment 
records sweat-related electrical conductance, not thoughts or diagnoses.

Following a seated acclimatisation and baseline period, participants will complete 
practice trials and then the dot-probe task while electrodermal activity is recorded

participants will be asked about discomfort, and a written debrief will explain the aims 
without claiming that individual scores diagnose anxiety. The session is expected to 
last circa 35 - 45 minutes.

Consent must be fully informed, given voluntarily, and recorded. Participants' data will

Access will be restricted, retention and deletion periods will follow university policy, and

Limitations  
The cross-sectional correlational design cannot demonstrate that sympathetic 
responding causes attentional bias; a shared process or unmeasured factor could 
explain an association. SCR is sensitive to temperature, movement, hydration, 
medication, caƯeine and individual diƯerences in responsivity. Standardisation and 
measured covariates can reduce but not eliminate these inﬂuences. The dot-probe task 
has documented reliability concerns, and a mean bias score may conceal dynamic 
vigilance and avoidance patterns. Adequate trials, prespeciﬁed exclusions and 
transparent reporting will mitigate, rather than remove, this limitation.  


## Page 9
Using static facial expressions may not fully reﬂect how people respond to emotions in 
real-life situations, and recruiting mainly university students and volunteers may limit 
how well the ﬁndings apply to wider populations. A sample size based on a medium 
interaction eƯect may not detect a smaller eƯect, so the power analysis and conﬁdence 
intervals will be important. Finally, measuring SCR while participants complete the task 
can show that the two occur at the same time, but it cannot show which causes the 
other. Future research could use eye tracking, more detailed measures of attentional 
bias, repeated testing, or experimental changes in arousal to examine the proposed 
mechanism more directly.
