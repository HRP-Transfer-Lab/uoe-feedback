[0014] [APA Body No Indent] Heart Rate Variability Recovery and Cognitive Reappraisal Success: Investigating Dynamic Cardiac Vagal Regulation During Emotion Regulation

[0015] [APA Level 2] Introduction

[0016] [Normal] Emotion regulation refers to the processes through which individuals modify the intensity, duration, or expression of their emotional responses (Gross, 1998; 2015). Cognitive reappraisal is one such strategy and involves changing the interpretation or meaning of an emotionally evocative situation in order to alter its emotional impact. For instance, participants may reinterpret an aversive scene as temporary, resolved, or less personally threatening (Gross, 1998; McRae, 2016). Reappraisal is generally considered an effective form of emotion regulation and has been associated with more adaptive emotional outcomes than less cognitively flexible strategies such as suppression (McRae, 2016).

[0017] [Normal] The capacity to regulate emotion is not exclusively psychological. Emotional challenges also involve changes in physiological systems that support attention, mobilisation, and subsequent recovery (Stange et al., 2017). Heart-rate variability (HRV) has therefore been proposed as a potential physiological correlate of self-regulation (Holzman & Bridgett, 2017). HRV refers to variation in the intervals between successive heartbeats (R-R intervals) and is commonly used to index aspects of autonomic cardiac regulation (Task Force of the European Society of Cardiology and the North American Society of Pacing and Electrophysiology, 1996). Short-term HRV can be quantified using the root mean square of successive differences between normal heartbeats (RMSSD), a time-domain measure commonly used as an index of parasympathetic cardiac regulation (Gfesser & Witte, 2026). HRV can be assessed under resting conditions as well as during and after emotional or cognitive tasks, allowing cardiac autonomic responding to be examined across regulatory contexts (Guendelman et al., 2024; Stange et al., 2017).

[0018] [APA Level 3] The Neurovisceral Integration Model

[0019] [Normal] The neurovisceral integration model provides a theoretical framework for understanding relations between cardiac autonomic regulation and emotion regulation (Thayer & Siegle, 2002; Thayer et al., 2012). The model proposes that cognitive, affective, and autonomic regulation are coordinated through an integrated network of cortical, subcortical, brainstem, and peripheral autonomic processes. Within this account, prefrontal systems involved in executive and emotional control are proposed to influence subcortical and brainstem pathways that shape sympathetic and parasympathetic cardiac output (Thayer & Lane, 2009; Thayer et al., 2012).

[0020] [Normal] The model proposes that adaptive functioning depends partly on flexible adjustment of behavioural and physiological responses to changing environmental demands. Vagal influences on the heart provide one pathway through which cardiac activity can change with such demands; HRV has consequently been proposed as a peripheral index of the functioning of this wider regulatory system (Thayer et al., 2009; 2012).

[0021] [Normal] Importantly, this account does not imply that HRV is a direct measure of prefrontal activity or an established biomarker of emotion-regulation ability. HRV is shaped by multiple physiological and behavioural processes and should therefore be interpreted as an indirect, context-sensitive physiological correlate of regulatory functioning (Thayer & Siegle, 2002; Thayer et al., 2012; Mulcahy et al., 2019).

[0022] [APA Level 3] Prior Findings

[0023] [Normal] Empirical research has linked HRV to several forms of cognitive, emotional, and behavioural self-regulation, although earlier findings have been mixed and the overall association appears modest. In a meta-analysis of 123 studies involving 14,347 participants, Holzman and Bridgett (2017) found only a small positive association between HRV and top-down self-regulation (r = .09), suggesting that HRV explains only a limited proportion of individual differences in regulatory performance.

[0024] [Normal] Importantly, resting and task-related HRV may reflect different aspects of regulation. Although higher resting HRV has been associated with more adaptive regulatory outcomes, task-related HRV appears to show more context-dependent associations, suggesting that resting and task HRV may reflect distinct aspects of regulatory functioning (Guendelman et al., 2024). Studies of active reappraisal have shown that HRV responses vary according to individual characteristics including neuroticism, rumination, and age (Simplicio et al., 2011; Nasso et al., 2019; Tupitsa et al., 2023). These findings challenge the assumption that higher HRV during active regulation consistently indicates greater regulatory success.

[0025] [Normal] More recent work suggests that the temporal pattern of cardiac responding may also be important. Rather than focusing exclusively on HRV at rest or during emotional challenge, studies have examined parasympathetic changes during subsequent recovery. Stange et al. (2017) found that, after a sad-film induction, greater parasympathetic flexibility – particularly vagal augmentation during a two-minute recovery period – was associated with greater attenuation of negative affect among participants who spontaneously used reappraisal or distraction. Jentsch and Wolf (2020) similarly found that, during a psychosocial stress paradigm, instructed reappraisal was associated with lower HRV during stress but stronger post-stress HRV recovery than suppression; this pattern was particularly evident among habitual reappraisers. These findings suggest that physiological recovery after regulatory demands have subsided may provide information not captured by HRV measured during the challenge alone.

[0026] [Normal] However, the available evidence does not establish how post-regulation HRV recovery relates specifically to successful instructed cognitive reappraisal. Stange et al. (2017) examined spontaneous strategy use after a film induction and found partial evidence compatible with the reverse temporal ordering, in which reappraisal-related affect reduction preceded greater parasympathetic recovery. Jentsch and Wolf (2020), meanwhile, used a psychosocial stressor rather than a standardised negative-image reappraisal paradigm. Thus, although existing findings identify recovery as a potentially important phase of autonomic regulation, it remains unclear whether greater post-reappraisal HRV recovery is associated with the degree to which individuals reduce negative affect during instructed cognitive reappraisal.

[0027] [APA Level 2] Rationale and Hypotheses

[0028] [Normal] Taken together, the existing literature suggests that HRV should not be interpreted solely as a static physiological characteristic measured at a single time point. The mixed findings for task-related HRV, alongside emerging evidence for the importance of post-challenge recovery, provide the rationale for examining HRV dynamically across cognitive reappraisal and recovery.

[0029] [Normal] The present study will examine cardiac activity during resting baseline, negative-image viewing, instructed cognitive reappraisal, and a standardised post-reappraisal recovery period. Reappraisal success will be operationalised independently of physiology as the within-participant reduction in self-reported negative affect from the view-negative to the reappraise-negative condition. Post-regulation recovery will be indexed by recovery-period log-transformed RMSSD (lnRMSSD) while statistically accounting for resting lnRMSSD and lnRMSSD during active reappraisal.

[0030] [Normal] The proposed study will therefore address the following research question:

[0031] [Normal] Is higher post-reappraisal recovery-period lnRMSSD, after accounting for resting and reappraisal-period lnRMSSD, associated with a greater within-participant reduction in self-reported negative affect?

[0032] [Normal] Based on this research question, the following confirmatory hypothesis is proposed:

[0033] [Normal] H1: Higher recovery-period lnRMSSD will be associated with greater reappraisal-related affect reduction after adjustment for resting lnRMSSD, reappraisal-period lnRMSSD, and pre-specified procedural covariates.

[0034] [Normal] Analyses examining HRV reactivity, habitual reappraisal, and alternative operationalisations of HRV recovery will be treated as exploratory.

[0035] [APA Level 2] Aims and Objectives

[0036] [Normal] The primary aim is to examine whether post-regulation HRV recovery is associated with successful instructed cognitive reappraisal in healthy young adults. Specifically, the study will (1) quantify HRV across baseline, emotional challenge, reappraisal, and recovery; (2) measure reappraisal success through reappraisal-related affect reduction; and (3) test whether HRV recovery predicts reappraisal success. Secondary analyses will explore HRV reactivity and potential moderation by resting HRV and habitual reappraisal.

[0037] [APA Level 1] Research Design

[0038] [APA Level 2] Design

[0039] [Normal] The study will use a laboratory-based, within-participant design. Participants will complete view-neutral and view-negative conditions in counterbalanced order, followed by a reappraise-negative condition and a standardised five-minute recovery period. ECG will be recorded continuously across a five-minute resting baseline, the experimental task, and recovery.

[0040] [Normal] The primary indicator of post-regulation recovery will be recovery-period lnRMSSD, interpreted while accounting for resting lnRMSSD and lnRMSSD during active reappraisal. Although reappraisal is experimentally instructed, the primary relationship of interest is correlational because HRV recovery itself is not manipulated.

[0041] [APA Level 2] Materials

[0042] [APA Level 2] Emotional stimuli will consist of standardised neutral and negative images from a validated image database available through the department. The task will include 24 neutral images and two matched sets of 24 negative images, allocated to the view-negative and reappraise-negative conditions in a counterbalanced manner. Negative affect will be rated after each image on a 0-100 visual analogue scale (0 = not at all negative; 100 = extremely negative). Reappraisal success will be calculated as the difference between mean view-negative and reappraise-negative ratings; an additional rating will be collected after recovery.

[0043] [Normal] Cardiac activity will be recorded continuously using a standardised ECG system. RMSSD will be calculated from cleaned R-R intervals and lnRMSSD for analysis. Respiration will be recorded continuously with a respiratory belt and included as a pre-specified covariate or sensitivity analysis because respiratory variation can influence RMSSD.

[0044] [Normal] A brief pre-task questionnaire will record sleep, caffeine, nicotine, alcohol, vigorous exercise, medication, and current mood (see Appendix C). Habitual reappraisal and suppression will be measured using the 10-item Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ; Gross & John, 2003), which comprises six cognitive-reappraisal items and four expressive-suppression items (see Appendix D). The ERQ has a replicable two-factor structure and showed good internal consistency for reappraisal (α = .89-.90) and acceptable-to-good consistency for suppression (α = .76-.80) in English-speaking community samples (Preece et al., 2019).

[0045] [APA Level 2] Participants

[0046] [Normal] The study will recruit healthy young adults aged 18-30 years through the university participant pool and campus advertising. An a priori power analysis in G*Power for linear multiple regression (fixed model, R² increase) indicated a minimum sample of 159 participants to detect a small incremental effect (f² = .05) of one tested predictor in a model containing five predictors, with α = .05 and power = .80. To account for withdrawals and unusable physiological data, approximately 190-200 participants will be recruited.

[0047] [Normal] Participants will be excluded if they report a relevant cardiovascular condition, medication substantially affecting cardiac autonomic activity, or acute illness on the testing day. Sufficient English proficiency to understand the task instructions will also be required. Age and gender will be recorded as demographic variables.

[0048] [APA Level 2] Data Analysis Plan

[0049] [Normal] Data will be analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics. Data will first be screened for missing values, errors, outliers, and insufficient ECG quality. Following preregistered artefact detection and correction procedures, RMSSD will be calculated for the relevant recording periods and log-transformed (lnRMSSD). Descriptive statistics will be calculated for demographic, physiological, and psychological variables.

[0050] [Normal] Negative-affect ratings will be analysed using a linear mixed-effects model with condition (view-neutral, view-negative, reappraise-negative) as a fixed effect and participant as a random intercept. Planned contrasts will test whether negative affect is higher during view-negative than view-neutral and lower during reappraise-negative than view-negative. The post-recovery negative-affect rating will be analysed descriptively as an exploratory secondary outcome.

[0051] [Normal] Reappraisal success will be calculated as mean negative affect during view-negative minus mean negative affect during reappraise-negative. The confirmatory analysis will use multiple linear regression with reappraisal success as the dependent variable and recovery-period lnRMSSD as the primary predictor. Resting lnRMSSD, reappraisal-period lnRMSSD, block order, and respiratory rate will be included as pre-specified covariates.

[0052] [Normal] Exploratory analyses will examine moderation by habitual reappraisal and resting HRV. A change-score measure of recovery (recovery lnRMSSD - reappraisal lnRMSSD) will be examined as a sensitivity analysis.

[0053] [Normal] All statistical tests will be conducted using an alpha level of .05.

[0054] [APA Level 2] Procedure

[0055] [APA Level 2] Following informed consent (see Appendices A & B), participants will complete the screening and pre-task questionnaire (see Appendix C). ECG and respiration equipment will then be fitted and signal quality checked. Participants will complete a five-minute resting baseline while seated, viewing a neutral fixation cross and breathing naturally.

[0056] [Normal] Standardised instructions and two to four practice trials will then be provided. During reappraisal, participants will be instructed to reinterpret negative images to reduce their emotional impact without relying on distraction or suppression. During view conditions, they will attend naturally without attempting to modify their emotional response.

[0057] [Normal] The view-neutral and view-negative blocks will be presented in counterbalanced order. The reappraise-negative block will always be presented last and will be followed immediately by the five-minute recovery period. Each trial will comprise a two-second fixation, two-second instruction cue, eight-second image presentation, affect rating of up to four seconds, and two-second inter-trial interval. A five-minute seated recovery period will immediately follow the reappraise-negative block while ECG recording continues. Participants will receive no further regulation instructions during recovery. A final affect rating, strategy-adherence and distress checks, and debriefing will complete the session.

[0058] [APA Level 2] Ethics

[0059] [Normal] Ethical approval will be obtained from the relevant institutional ethics committee before recruitment begins. Participants will provide informed consent and will be informed that participation is voluntary and that they may withdraw without penalty.

[0060] [Normal] The primary ethical concern is the possibility of temporary emotional distress caused by exposure to negative images. Participants will therefore receive appropriate information about the nature of the task, may pause or terminate the experiment at any point, and will receive a debriefing following participation. Appropriate support information will be provided if required by institutional policy. Procedures will follow British Psychological Society (BPS, 2021) guidance for minimising psychological harm.

[0061] [Normal] ECG recording is non-invasive; however, physiological data will be treated as sensitive personal data. Data will be pseudonymised, with identifying information stored separately from research data. Access will be restricted to authorised members of the research team, and data retention and deletion will follow institutional requirements and applicable data-protection legislation.

[0062] [APA Level 2] Limitations

[0063] [Normal] Several limitations should be acknowledged. As HRV recovery is not experimentally manipulated, associations with reappraisal success cannot establish causality or direction of effect; successful emotion regulation may itself contribute to subsequent physiological recovery. HRV is also influenced by respiration, caffeine, sleep, physical activity, and time of day, which can be standardised or documented but not completely controlled. Finally, self-reported affect may be affected by demand characteristics, habituation, or use of alternative strategies such as distraction. Respiratory monitoring, strategy-adherence checks, counterbalanced image sets, and sensitivity analyses will reduce, but cannot eliminate, these alternative explanations.

[0252] [Normal] Title of the study: Heart Rate Variability Recovery and Cognitive Reappraisal Success: Investigating Dynamic Cardiac Vagal Regulation During Emotion Regulation

[0255] [Normal] What was the purpose of the research?

[0256] [Normal] This research is part of a postgraduate research project examining the relationship between heart-rate variability (HRV) and cognitive emotion regulation in healthy young adults. Specifically, the study investigates whether physiological recovery following cognitive reappraisal of negative emotional images is associated with a greater reduction in self-reported negative affect. The aim is to better understand how changes in cardiac regulation during and following emotional challenges relate to individual differences in successful emotion regulation.

[0257] [Normal] What did I complete and what did the study aim to find out?

[0258] [Normal] During the study, your cardiac activity was recorded using electrocardiography (ECG) while you completed an emotion-regulation task. You viewed both neutral and negative emotional images and were asked either to view the images naturally or to reinterpret negative images in a way that reduced their emotional impact. After each image, you rated how negatively you felt. Your cardiac activity was also recorded during a resting baseline and a recovery period following the task.

[0259] [Normal] The purpose of these measures is to examine whether changes in heart-rate variability (HRV) following cognitive reappraisal are associated with successful emotion regulation. Reappraisal success will be assessed by comparing negative-affect ratings when viewing negative images naturally with ratings when actively reappraising negative images. HRV will be examined across the resting, reappraisal, and recovery periods to investigate whether greater post-regulation physiological recovery is associated with a greater reduction in negative affect.
