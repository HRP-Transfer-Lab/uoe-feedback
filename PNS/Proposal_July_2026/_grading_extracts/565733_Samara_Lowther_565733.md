[0042] [normal] Abstract

[0045] [normal] The purpose of this research proposal is to examine whether threat-related and/or deprivation-related childhood adversities are associated with young adults' neural responses to emotional threat in a different manner. In order to evaluate the relationship between threat-related and/or deprivation-related childhood adversities and neural responses to emotional threat, EEG (Electroencephalography) will be employed to measure Event-Related Potentials (ERPs). ERPs are electrical signals produced by the brain in response to a stimulus.

[0046] [normal] Specifically, the Late Positive Potential (LPP) is an ERP component that has been shown to reflect sustained attention to emotionally salient stimuli. Childhood adversity will be assessed using a validated childhood adversity instrument. Threat-related childhood adversity and deprivation-related childhood adversity will be collected as two separate variables. Threat-related adversity is expected to correlate with LPP responses to threat stimuli whereas deprivation-related adversity is not expected to correlate in the same manner. A correlational design will be employed within the present study. Multiple regression will be used to determine the contribution of the two adversity types to the LPP responses to threat stimuli. The overall goal of the present study is to further understand the relationship between childhood adversity and the neural processing of threat stimuli in the adult brain.

[0047] [normal] Keywords: childhood adversity, threat, deprivation, EEG, Late Positive Potential, emotional threat

[0108] [Title] Threat and Deprivation in Childhood Adversity: Associations with Neural Responses to Emotional Threat in Young Adults

[0115] [normal] Background

[0117] [normal] Childhood adversity refers to experiences during childhood that are both stressful and can have long-lasting effects on the emotional and psychological development of those who experience them. However, there are many different types of adversity that can affect development differently. One of the major differences between types of childhood adversity is between threat-related adversity and deprivation-related adversity. Threat-related adversity refers to experiences that result in harm or threats of harm, such as sexual, physical, or emotional abuse. Deprivation-related adversity, on the other hand, refers to the lack of expected environmental input in the development of a child, such as neglect of any form. (Sheridan & McLaughlin, 2014). Distinguishing between these dimensions may be important when investigating how childhood adversity is associated with emotional processing later in life, rather than treating all adverse experiences as having the same effects (McLaughlin et al., 2014).

[0120] [normal] Prior findings

[0122] [normal] One way of investigating differences in emotional processing is through electroencephalography (EEG), which can be used to measure event-related potentials (ERPs) associated with responses to specific stimuli. One ERP component of relevance to this study is the Late Positive Potential (LPP). Studies have shown that the LPP is associated with sustained attention to emotionally significant information (Hajcak et al., 2010). Furthermore, the amplitudes of the LPP are greater in response to emotionally arousing compared to neutral stimuli, making it an appropriate component to use to examine individual differences in the emotional processing of the participants (Hajcak & Olvet, 2008).

[0123] [normal] Previous research has also linked childhood maltreatment with differences in LPP responses to threatening facial expressions. Sandre et al. (2018) found that adults with a history of childhood maltreatment showed increased LPP responses as fearful facial expressions increased in emotional intensity. However, findings across the wider ERP literature remain inconsistent, with recent review evidence suggesting that a clear and consistent relationship between retrospectively reported childhood adversity and ERP responses has not yet been established (Hedrick et al., 2024).

[0126] [normal] Theory

[0128] [normal] Threat-related childhood adversity may alter emotional threat processing by increasing attention towards potential signs of danger (Sheridan & McLaughlin, 2014). In contrast, deprivation involves reduced expected social and environmental input and may influence development through different neurodevelopmental mechanisms (McLaughlin et al., 2014). These differences suggest that threat and deprivation may have distinct associations with emotional processing later in life.

[0131] [normal] Rationale and Hypothesis

[0133] [normal] Although previous research suggests that childhood maltreatment may be associated with altered LPP responses to threatening facial expressions, much of this work has treated childhood maltreatment as a broad category or has focused primarily on abuse-related experiences. For example, Sandre et al. (2018) demonstrated altered LPP responses in adults with histories of childhood maltreatment, but did not directly compare threat-related and deprivation-related adversity as distinct dimensions.

[0135] [normal] This study therefore addresses the research question: Are threat-related and deprivation-related childhood adversities differently associated with LPP responses to emotional threat in young adults?

[0137] [normal] It is hypothesised that greater threat-related childhood adversity will be associated with larger LPP responses to emotional threat. In comparison, deprivation-related adversity is expected to show a weaker association with LPP responses.

[0140] [normal] H1: Greater threat-related childhood adversity will be associated with larger LPP responses to emotional threat.

[0142] [normal] H2: Deprivation-related childhood adversity will show a weaker association with LPP responses to emotional threat than threat-related adversity.

[0144] [normal] The null hypothesis is that there will be no significant association between threat-related or deprivation-related childhood adversity and LPP responses to emotional threat.

[0146] [normal] Aims and Objectives

[0148] [normal] The study aims to investigate the independent associations of threat-related and deprivation-related childhood adversity with LPP responses to emotional threat in young adults, and to determine whether threat shows a stronger association with LPP amplitude than deprivation.

[0152] [normal] Method

[0154] [normal] Participants

[0156] [normal] Participants will be young adults aged 18 to 30. A power analysis suggests that around 68 participants would be needed to give the study a reasonable chance of detecting a meaningful relationship between the variables. Participants will need to have normal vision and no known neurological condition that could affect the EEG recording. Recruitment will aim to include people with a range of different childhood adversity experiences.

[0158] [normal] Design

[0160] [normal] A quantitative, cross-sectional correlational design will be used. Threat and deprivation scores will be continuous predictor variables, while the primary outcome will be LPP amplitude in response to emotional threat. This design allows the independent associations of both adversity dimensions with LPP responses to be examined.

[0164] [normal] Materials

[0166] [normal] Childhood adversity will be assessed using the Deprivation and Threat–Adult Self-report (DT-AS), a validated retrospective measure designed to assess childhood threat and deprivation as separate dimensions in young adults. The measure contains a 33-item Threat scale assessing experiences involving harm or potential harm, and a 30-item Deprivation scale assessing reduced or absent expected cognitive, physical and socio-emotional experiences. Separate continuous scores will be calculated for threat and deprivation, with higher scores reflecting greater exposure to each dimension of childhood adversity (Tsai et al., 2026).

[0167] [normal] Emotional threat processing will be assessed using a computer-based facial-expression task adapted from Sandre et al. (2018). Participants will view neutral, fearful and angry facial expressions taken from the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces database (KDEF). Neutral expressions will be morphed with fearful and angry expressions to create different levels of emotional intensity (25%, 50%, 75% and 100%), allowing responses to both ambiguous and unambiguous threat cues to be examined. EEG will be recorded during the task, and the Late Positive Potential (LPP) will be measured at the Pz electrode between 400 and 1,000 ms after stimulus onset, consistent with the procedure used by Sandre et al. (2018).

[0169] [normal] Procedure

[0171] [normal] Following informed consent, participants will complete a demographic questionnaire and the DT-AS. An EEG cap will then be fitted, and participants will view neutral, fearful and angry facial expressions at varying emotional intensities while EEG activity is recorded. Following the task, participants will be debriefed and provided with relevant support information.

[0174] [normal] Ethics

[0176] [normal] Ethical approval will be obtained before data collection. Participants will receive an information sheet and provide informed consent, with clear information regarding the sensitive nature of the childhood-adversity questions and their right to decline questions or withdraw in accordance with the approved procedure. To minimise distress, unnecessary detailed descriptions of adverse experiences will not be requested, and participants will receive a debrief containing appropriate support information. Data will be stored securely and identified using participant codes rather than names, in accordance with relevant ethical guidance.

[0180] [normal] Data Preparation and Analysis Plan

[0182] [normal] EEG data will be preprocessed to identify and manage artefacts from eye movements, blinking, muscle activity and other sources of noise. Trials containing excessive artefacts will be excluded. The LPP will be quantified at Pz between 400 and 1,000 ms after stimulus onset, with the primary outcome defined as the difference in mean LPP amplitude between threatening and neutral facial-expression trials.

[0183] [normal] Descriptive statistics will summarise threat, deprivation and LPP scores. Assumptions including normality, linearity, homoscedasticity and multicollinearity will be assessed before inferential analysis.

[0184] [normal] Multiple regression will examine the independent associations of threat and deprivation with LPP amplitude. Both adversity dimensions will be entered simultaneously as continuous predictors. A significant positive coefficient for threat would support H1. To test H2, the standardised regression coefficients for threat and deprivation will be compared, with a significantly larger threat coefficient supporting the hypothesis. Statistical significance will be evaluated at p < .05, with effect sizes and confidence intervals reported where appropriate.

[0187] [normal] Expected Results

[0189] [normal] Threat-related adversity is expected to show a significant positive association with LPP amplitude, such that greater threat exposure is associated with larger neural responses to emotional threat. Deprivation is expected to show a weaker association. A significantly larger standardised regression coefficient for threat than deprivation would support H2. Findings that do not follow this pattern would suggest that relationships between childhood adversity dimensions and neural threat processing are more complex than predicted.

[0192] [normal] Discussion

[0193] [normal] Overview

[0195] [normal] If the predicted results are observed, they would suggest that threat and deprivation have partly distinct associations with neural threat processing.

[0197] [normal] Comparison with Research

[0198] [normal] The findings would be consistent with Sandre et al. (2018), who reported heightened LPP responses to fearful facial expressions following childhood maltreatment, while extending this work by examining threat and deprivation separately. They may also help clarify inconsistencies identified across the ERP literature (Hedrick et al., 2024).

[0200] [normal] Theoretical Interpretations

[0201] [normal] A stronger threat–LPP association would support models proposing that threatening childhood environments increase sensitivity to potential danger, whereas deprivation influences development through different mechanisms (McLaughlin et al., 2014; Sheridan & McLaughlin, 2014).

[0203] [normal] Research Evaluation

[0204] [normal] Limitations include retrospective self-report and the frequent co-occurrence of threat and deprivation. The correlational design prevents causal conclusions, while EEG provides limited spatial localisation and may be affected by physiological artefacts.

[0207] [normal] Implications

[0208] [normal] The findings could support distinguishing between dimensions of childhood adversity rather than treating adversity as a single construct.

[0209] [normal] Future Directions

[0210] [normal] Future studies could use larger, more diverse samples, longitudinal designs and additional neuroimaging methods to examine how these associations develop over time.

[0211] [normal] Conclusion

[0212] [normal] This study will examine whether threat and deprivation show different associations with LPP responses to emotional threat. The findings may improve understanding of how specific forms of childhood adversity relate to neural emotional processing.
