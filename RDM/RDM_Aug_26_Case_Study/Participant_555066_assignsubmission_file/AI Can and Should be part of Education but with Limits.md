AI Can and Should Be Part of Education but with Limits

As artificial intelligence has become part of everyday life, it has also begun to change how students learn. AI can explain difficult ideas in different ways, suggest examples, summarise information, identify errors and provide feedback within seconds. It can even give students a private space to ask the questions they may feel embarrassed to ask a teacher, such as the awkward “I still don’t understand this.” However, the same technology can write essays, produce convincing but inaccurate information and make it easier for students to avoid the thinking that education is supposed to develop.

This creates a difficult debate. Schools and universities are often presented with two opposing choices: embrace AI because it is the future or restrict it because it threatens learning. But treating the issue as a simple choice between accepting AI and banning it misses an important point. The more useful question is not whether AI is entirely good or bad for education, but under what conditions it can improve learning without replacing the thinking, judgement and human interaction that make education valuable.

Current evidence points towards a balanced answer. AI can have a valuable place in education, but its integration requires clear boundaries, human oversight and stronger AI literacy among both students and educators.

AI can be a useful learning tool

There are good reasons for taking educational AI seriously. In 2025, Shuzhen Chen and Alan Cheung reviewed 57 studies and found that generative AI had an overall positive effect on university students learning outcomes. The benefits included improvements in academic achievement, language skills, motivation and higher-order thinking. However, AI did not have a significant effect on metacognition, and the benefits varied depending on the learner, the technology used and the educational context.

Other large reviews point in a similar direction. In a 2025 meta-analysis, Xiaohong Liu and colleagues analysed 49 studies involving school and higher-education students and found positive average effects on learning achievement and motivation. Importantly, these effects varied according to factors such as educational level, subject, type of AI interface and length of intervention.

This matters because students do not all learn at the same pace or struggle with the same things. A student who does not understand a concept can ask AI for another explanation, request examples or receive immediate feedback. In a classroom with many students, one teacher cannot realistically provide continuous individual support to everyone. AI could therefore help fill some of that gap rather than replace the teacher.

However, these findings need an important qualification: better performance with AI does not necessarily mean better independent learning. For example, a student who uses AI to understand a difficult theory and then develops their own argument is learning differently from a student who asks AI to produce the argument for them. Both use the same technology, but only one is using it mainly to support their own thinking.

Research showing improved performance with AI assistance cannot automatically demonstrate that students have developed stronger skills when that assistance is removed.

This distinction is particularly important because a 2024 systematic review by Chunpeng Zhai and colleagues examined 14 studies and found that over-reliance on AI dialogue systems was associated with negative effects on cognitive abilities including critical thinking, analytical reasoning and decision-making. However, the review was based on a relatively small body of literature, so the findings should still be interpreted cautiously. It would therefore be too strong to conclude that AI inevitably damages thinking in every context.

The more defensible conclusion is that how students use AI matters. Using it to clarify, practise and receive feedback may support learning but repeatedly using it to replace analysis and argument construction may reduce opportunities to practise those abilities.

The problem of cheating

Academic integrity is one of the strongest arguments against generative AI. Students can now produce convincing written work quickly, creating an obvious problem for assessments designed to measure independent knowledge and reasoning.

In their 2024 article, Debby Cotton and colleagues examine this challenge and argue that ChatGPT creates significant academic-integrity concerns, while recommending institutional policies, training, support and appropriate strategies to prevent misuse.

The concern is legitimate, but it does not follow that every educational use of AI should therefore be prohibited. Asking AI to write an assessed essay is fundamentally different from asking it to explain a concept, generate practice questions or provide feedback on an idea. Treating every use as equivalent creates an unnecessary choice between unrestricted AI and a complete ban.

A better response is to connect the rules to the purpose of an assessment. Some tasks may need to prohibit AI completely because the skill being assessed must be demonstrated independently. Others could permit limited and disclosed assistance, while assessments could also ask students to evaluate an AI-generated answer, identify its weaknesses and defend their own reasoning.

In this way, education can protect academic integrity while teaching students something increasingly important: how to judge AI rather than simply how to use it.

Privacy, fairness and reliability concerns

Learning outcomes and cheating are not the only concerns.

In their 2023 guidance for UNESCO, Fengchun Miao and Wayne Holmes call for a human-centred approach to generative AI in education, highlighting the need for data privacy protection, appropriate regulation and age-sensitive use. They argue that educational uses of generative AI should be ethical, safe, equitable and meaningful.

These responsibilities cannot simply be left to individual students. Students may enter written work, questions or personal information into AI systems without fully understanding how that information may be handled. Schools and universities therefore have a responsibility to understand the technologies they recommend and establish clear boundaries around the information students should provide.

Fairness also matters. AI will not necessarily benefit everyone equally. Differences in access to technology, digital skills and knowledge about effective AI use could reproduce or even widen existing educational inequalities.

Reliability creates another problem. Generative AI can provide information that sounds confident even when it is inaccurate. This means students need more than instructions on writing an effective prompt. They need to know how to verify claims, locate credible sources, recognise possible bias and identify when an answer should not be trusted.

Stories about an AI inventing a reference or a student cheating with ChatGPT are concerning, but isolated examples cannot tell us whether AI is harmful to education overall. The opposite reasoning is equally weak: one successful AI-supported lesson cannot demonstrate that AI will improve education everywhere. The available research instead suggests a more cautious conclusion: benefits and risks vary according to how, where and why the technology is used.

The answer is AI literacy, not AI dependency

If AI is going to be part of a student life, education should prepare them to use it intelligently.

AI literacy needs to mean more than knowing how to write effective prompts. Students should learn to question AI-generated information, check factual claims against reliable sources, recognise possible bias and understand that an answer can sound convincing without necessarily being accurate. However, AI literacy should not be treated as the solution to every problem associated with AI. Luci Pangrazio (2026) questions this assumption, arguing that AI literacy is sometimes presented as a “cure-all” despite the complexity and constantly changing nature of AI. This suggests that teaching students how to use AI critically is important, but responsibility cannot be placed on students alone.

Schools and universities also need to decide when AI supports learning and when it begins to replace the skills students are expected to develop. Rather than relying only on AI detection, assessments could make students’ reasoning more visible through drafts, reflections, source evaluation and opportunities to explain their conclusions. AI should support rather than replace human judgement, with students responsible for evaluating AI feedback and educators responsible for ensuring that AI-assisted materials are accurate and appropriate. AI literacy should therefore be part of a wider educational response, not a substitute for institutional responsibility.

Education should adapt to AI without surrendering to it

The evidence does not support a simple conclusion that AI is either good or bad for education. Research suggests genuine benefits for learning, but these vary across students, technologies and educational contexts. At the same time, concerns about over-reliance, academic integrity, misinformation, privacy and unequal access cannot be ignored. Together, this evidence supports a more balanced approach: conditional integration.

Schools and universities should use AI when it serves a clear educational purpose, while limiting its use when it replaces the skills students are expected to develop or demonstrate. This requires teacher oversight, protection of student data, assessments that make reasoning visible, and teaching students to question AI-generated information rather than simply accept it.

Ultimately, education has always involved learning how to use information without giving up our own judgement, and AI makes this ability even more important. The real question is not simply whether students use AI, but whether they finish the learning process with greater understanding, better judgement and a stronger ability to think for themselves. The role of AI in education should therefore be judged by how well it supports these abilities without replacing them.

Methods

This article was developed using the critical-thinking principles described by Halpern and Dunn (2023), including evaluating the quality of evidence, identifying assumptions, considering alternative explanations and ensuring that conclusions did not go beyond what the evidence could support. Toulmin’s (1958) model was also used to structure the argument through claims, grounds, warrants, qualifiers and rebuttals; for example, evidence showing that AI can improve academic performance was not treated as proof that it also improves independent learning, as this would require the additional assumption that better performance with AI reflects stronger independent ability. Evidence both for and against AI integration was considered to reduce myside bias, which can lead people to evaluate arguments supporting their existing beliefs more favourably (Stanovich & West, 2008), while a false dichotomy between unrestricted AI use and complete prohibition was avoided by proposing conditional integration as an alternative. The Elaboration Likelihood Model also informed how the argument was presented, as central-route persuasion involves careful consideration of arguments relevant to an issue (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). The article therefore aimed to persuade readers through evidence and reasoning rather than relying mainly on emotional or peripheral cues, while accessible language and practical examples were used to make the discussion suitable for a public audience. Overall, the conclusion was based on the available evidence, appropriately qualified and developed through consideration of counterarguments rather than presenting one side of the debate as unquestionably correct.
