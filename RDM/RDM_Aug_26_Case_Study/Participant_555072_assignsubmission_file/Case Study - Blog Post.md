What If Vegetarianism Isn’t Actually the Point?

Vegetarian or meat-eater? Sometimes, it feels as though these are the only two options. I’ve always found this debate oddly difficult to have a sensible conversation about. One side discusses the health benefits of cutting out meat; the other argues that humans have been eating meat since the dawn of time. Somewhere along the road, the question about what we eat became tied to what kind of person we are.

But perhaps we are starting with the wrong question.

To call someone “vegetarian” tells us about what they don’t eat, but not much about what they do eat. Anyone can avoid meat whilst heavily consuming highly processed foods and refined carbohydrates, in the same way a meat eater can have a diet based largely on vegetables, whole grains, nuts, pulses and other minimally processed foods.

This matters because when we ask whether vegetarianism is “better” than an omnivorous diet, often we are comparing labels rather than the diets themselves.

So, before a side is chosen, maybe we should ask something more beneficial: what does the evidence actually say about eating more plants, consuming less meat, and what happens when one replaces the other?

I’m afraid the answer is more nuanced than either side of the vegetarian debate would prefer: we do not all need to be vegetarian, but there are compelling reasons for eating more plants and carefully thinking about what replaces the meat we remove.

Now, is vegetarianism actually healthier?

Vegetarianism has earned its reputation for being healthy for good reason. The evidence is not just a group of wellness influencers guiding us to eat more vegetables. A 2024 umbrella review by Landry and colleagues brought together 21 systematic reviews studying vegetarian and vegan dietary patterns and cardiovascular health. Generally, lower rates of cardiovascular disease were associated with vegetarian diets, whilst vegan diets were linked to improvements in many cardiovascular risk factors.

That seems pretty convincing and is definitely evidence worth taking seriously.

However, there is a crucial catch: a lot of the evidence from the general public was observational. Researchers found people with vegetarian diets experience better health outcomes, but that does not mean the diet itself is what caused those outcomes. Vegetarians and non-vegetarians can differ in other ways, such as smoking, exercise, healthcare use, alcohol consumption or other aspects of their lifestyle.

At this point, another type of evidence becomes helpful.

Wang et al. (2023) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis focused solely on randomised controlled trials, in which participants were given vegetarian or comparison diets. Across 20 trials with 1,878 participants who had cardiovascular disease, diabetes or several cardiovascular risk factors, vegetarian diets were associated with reductions in LDL cholesterol, HbA1c and body weight.

The case for vegetarian diets is strengthened by this evidence, as randomisation gives researchers more confidence that the intervention contributed to the outcome.

Although even this evidence has its limitations. The average intervention was around six months, and the people were not representative of the general population; they already had diabetes, cardiovascular disease or elevated risks. Therefore, this research suggests that vegetarian diets can improve certain cardiometabolic risk factors in specific groups. It does not tell us that being vegetarian makes every healthy person healthier over the next 20 years.

And this distinction matters.

The evidence provides a legitimate health case for vegetarianism. What it does not give is a universal ruling.

What are you replacing your meat with?

Now the vegetarian argument gets more interesting. Just taking the meat off your plate is not, on its own, a dietary intervention. Something will need to replace it.

López-Moreno et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis examining 36 randomised controlled trials comparing red meat with various sources of protein and other foods. Researchers found that replacing red meat with plant protein was linked to lower total and LDL cholesterol. At first, this sounds like another tally for vegetarianism, but dig further and the conclusion becomes more nuanced. The evidence’s certainty for the outcomes was assessed as low to very low. The results are promising; however, they’re not strong enough to reinforce the idea that replacing every animal food with plant food will automatically improve our health.

Importantly, the study forces us to acknowledge a question the vegetarian vs omnivore discussion often ignores: what exactly are we eating instead?

Now imagine having steak for dinner and replacing it with vegetables, lentils and whole grains. That is one dietary change; however, replacing it with highly processed foods and refined carbohydrates is another. Either meal would bring you closer to a “meat-free” diet, but it would be hard to argue that they are nutritionally equivalent.

Being labelled “vegetarian” provides us with little information about the diet itself. The potential benefit might not simply be from the lack of meat; it might come from the higher-quality foods replacing it. The idea is not to find one exact replacement, but to consider the nutritional quality of what replaces what.

Maybe the better question instead of “How do I stop eating meat?” should be “What could I eat instead?”

What if the evidence doesn’t agree?

Once we find evidence that supports a plant-based diet, there is an impulse to stop searching for more. However, that would make the argument easier, not stronger.

The evidence about red meat gives us a major complication. Zeraatkar et al. (2019) conducted a systematic review of randomised trials examining whether consuming less red meat improves cardiometabolic and cancer outcomes. Across 12 eligible trials, the search resulted in little or no difference in various major health outcomes between lower and higher red-meat intake.

This could shift the debate in the other direction. If reducing red meat does not consistently improve health outcomes, why bother changing our diets?

Yet we need another look at the evidence.

The certainty of the evidence was rated low by the authors (Zeraatkar et al., 2019). One large study involving almost 49,000 women gave much of the evidence, but it was not designed specifically to test the effects of reducing red meat. Rather, it involved a wider low-fat dietary intervention in which red-meat intake was decreased.

This matters because a study can be large without actually being the perfect test of the question we are asking.

Therefore, this evidence does not prove that consuming less red meat has no health benefits. Equally, it stops us from stating that simply cutting out red meat will significantly reduce everyone’s risk of disease.

In between is where the most honest conclusion resides. The evidence is promising for replacing red meat with plant protein, whilst the evidence that just eating less red meat automatically produces major health improvements is less convincing.

How about the planet?

The popularity of vegetarianism isn’t only due to health; there is also the environmental argument. The evidence here usually points the same way: diets with less animal-source foods generally have lower environmental impacts, particularly in terms of greenhouse-gas emissions.

However, even here the details matter.

Kalmpourtzidou et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review examining 703 diets across 41 countries and found significant variation in environmental impacts depending on location, the foods included and the environmental measure being used. The most common outcome measured was carbon footprint, whilst other measures, such as biodiversity and freshwater use, were assessed less frequently. Researchers also found that some meat substitutes had higher carbon footprints than other plant-based options.

This doesn’t mean plant-based and meat-heavy diets have the same environmental impacts. It means that simply calling a diet “sustainable” can hide a lot of complexity.

If we really want to make environmentally responsible choices, we need to question what impact we are measuring and which foods we compare. For example, a lentil-based meal is not environmentally equivalent to every plant-based marketed product.

Yet again, the label tells us less than the contents of the plate.

Vegetarianism doesn’t mean the same thing everywhere.

We need to be cautious for one more reason when treating vegetarianism as a universal answer: food is cultural.

A useful example comes from India. Corichi (2021), reporting on a Pew Research Centre survey of nearly 30,000 Indian adults, found that 39% identified as vegetarian, whilst 81% reported restricting meat in some way. The difference matters. The label captured dietary identity instead of providing a precise nutritional description because the survey allowed people to define “vegetarian” themselves.

Research into Indian dietary patterns also shows significant variation regionally. Green et al. (2016) found a variety of dietary patterns across the country, including vegetarian patterns alongside patterns with meat, sweets and other foods.

To put it another way, even in a country where vegetarianism is deeply linked with cultural and religious practices, there is no single “Indian vegetarian diet.”

This doesn’t mean culture should guide what everyone eats. It merely reminds us that dietary advice does not exist in isolation. It will vary from person to person what is affordable, practical and culturally meaningful.

Therefore, good recommendations need to consider more than whether meat is on the plate.

So, should we all become vegetarian?

Not necessarily. The evidence provides us with valid reasons to consume more plant-based foods and to consider replacing some animal foods with high-quality plant alternatives. It does not state, however, that vegetarianism is a universal solution or that simply removing meat guarantees better health.

Maybe the more useful question is not whether we are vegetarian or omnivorous, but what our diet actually contains. What are we eating, what are we replacing, and does it make sense for our environment, health and context?

Perhaps vegetarianism was never the point. Perhaps eating better is.

Methods

To write this blog post, I used Halpern and Dunn’s (2023) critical-thinking principles, in particular the examination of evidence, identification of assumptions, consideration of alternative explanations, and evaluation of whether conclusions can be generalised. I applied these concepts when comparing randomised and observational evidence, taking study populations and limitations into account, and assessing evidence that contradicted my initial position. To structure my argument, I also used aspects of the Toulmin Method of Argumentation, including a clear claim, grounds, rebuttals and qualifiers (THUNK, 2016). This guided me to create a qualified position instead of offering vegetarianism as a universally superior diet. The overall evidence was evaluated in connection to health, environmental and cultural contexts, and counterevidence regarding red-meat consumption was purposefully included to test the strength of my argument.
