Online Learning Won't Replace the Classroom — And That's a Good Thing

When I think about education, my first instinct is to picture a classroom: a lecturer explaining an idea, students asking questions and conversations continuing long after the lesson has finished. But is that my preference, or is it evidence that traditional education is better?

That is the question we should be asking.

The debate about online versus traditional education is often presented as a choice between one and the other. That is the wrong question. Instead, we should ask: What can online education do better, what does face-to-face education do better, and how can we combine the two?

For me, the answer is blended learning.

There is no denying that online education has transformed what is possible. A student can attend a lecture from home, watch a recording around work or caring responsibilities, and access learning materials without travelling to campus. For students who cannot easily relocate or commute, this flexibility can make higher education more accessible. Furthermore, it also opens the door to students like myself, parents who cannot afford to stop working but who want to continue pursuing a dream or a better future.

Research also challenges the assumption that online learning is automatically inferior. A meta-analysis of 51 effect sizes found that blended learning was associated with better academic performance than traditional classroom instruction, although the effect size varied by discipline (Cao, 2023). So, if I automatically assume that students learn better simply because they are sitting in a classroom, I am making the same mistake that I would criticise in someone who assumes online learning is always superior.

But flexibility is not the same as quality.

The pandemic demonstrated this rather clearly. When universities moved online, many students benefited from digital resources, but others struggled with unreliable internet, unsuitable devices or a lack of quiet space. The Office for Students found that 52% of surveyed students said slow or unreliable internet affected their learning. In comparison, 71% reported being affected by a lack of quiet study space (Office for Students, 2020).

That matters because saying "everyone can study online" assumes that everyone has the same circumstances. They do not.

Another issue we sometimes overlook is that education is not just about receiving information.

A good university experience involves discussion, collaboration, feedback and relationships (Schürmann et al., 2023). It is often easier to ask a lecturer a spontaneous question in a classroom than to type an email and wait for a response. Students can also build friendships and a sense of belonging more easily when they meet regularly.

The Office for Students' review of blended learning found that some students reported lower-quality or less timely feedback online and feelings of isolation because of reduced peer networks and academic community. This does not prove that traditional education is always better, but it does challenge the assumption that convenience should be our only measure of educational quality (Altindag et al., 2021).

This is where critical thinking becomes important.

If I only search for evidence supporting my preferred way of learning, I am demonstrating confirmation bias. Halpern argues that critical thinking requires us to deliberately evaluate evidence, consider alternatives and seek information that might challenge our existing beliefs (Halpern & Dunn, 2021). That means I should be willing to ask: What if online learning works better than I expected? What if traditional teaching has weaknesses I have overlooked? What assumptions am I making?

The answer is uncomfortable but useful: both approaches have strengths and weaknesses.

That is why I reject the idea that universities must choose between completely online education and traditional classroom teaching. This is a false dichotomy. A third option exists: combine the strengths of both (Harper et al., 2024).

Imagine a university course where students can watch introductory lectures online at a convenient time, use digital resources to revise and complete independent activities remotely, but still attend campus for seminars, practical sessions, group discussions and opportunities to speak directly with lecturers.

That does not make the classroom obsolete. It makes classroom time more valuable.

Instead of using face-to-face sessions to deliver information students could access online, universities could use them for what humans do particularly well: questioning, debating, solving problems, collaborating and building relationships.

Of course, blended learning is not automatically successful. The Office for Students has warned that poor-quality online resources, inadequate digital infrastructure and insufficient support can undermine the student experience. Universities therefore need to invest not just in technology but also in staff training, student support and meaningful interaction (Bond et al., 2020).

Ultimately, the future of education should not be about choosing between a laptop and a classroom.

It should be about asking a better question: What combination gives students the best opportunity to learn?

My answer is blended education. Online learning gives us flexibility, accessibility, and new ways to access knowledge. Traditional education gives us human interaction, community and opportunities for immediate discussion and feedback.

Good education should not force us to choose between the two.

It should give us the best of both.

Methods

In this blog, I apply critical thinking concepts from Halpern and Unit 6. Halpern's distinction between System 1 and System 2 thinking helps me distinguish between an immediate intuition that traditional classroom education is superior and a more deliberate, evidence-based evaluation of the issue (Halpern & Dunn, 2021). I deliberately consider evidence supporting both online and traditional education, reflecting Halpern's emphasis on seeking contradictory evidence and developing cognitive flexibility to reduce confirmation or myside bias (Stanovich et al., 2013). I also identify and challenge assumptions, such as the belief that online education is inherently more accessible or that face-to-face education is necessarily more effective. Drawing on Unit 6's approach to argumentation, I identify premises, assumptions, evidence, counterarguments, and a conclusion. I further employ the Toulmin Model by connecting the central claim to supporting evidence and reasoning, while acknowledging limitations and counterarguments (Gülen & Dönmez, 2024).

Finally, I challenge the false dichotomy of choosing either online or traditional education and propose blended learning as a reasoned alternative (Lu, 2021). This approach demonstrates open-minded evaluation by considering multiple perspectives before reaching a conclusion, rather than simply defending an initial preference (Halpern & Dunn, 2023).
