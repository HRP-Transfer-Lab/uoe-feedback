Banning AI Won’t Save Education — Teaching Students How to Think With It Might

Artificial intelligence is already part of university life. Students use it to explain difficult concepts, summarise readings, plan essays and check their work. According to the Higher Education Policy Institute, 95% of UK undergraduates surveyed in 2026 reported using AI in at least one way, while 94% said they used generative AI to help with assessed work (Stephenson & Armstrong, 2026). At that point, telling students simply not to use AI starts to look less like a solution and more like avoiding the problem.

As a university student, I can see why both sides of the debate are appealing. AI can make learning quicker and more accessible, but it can also make it dangerously easy to avoid the difficult thinking that learning requires. The debate is therefore often framed as a choice: universities either ban AI to protect academic standards, or accept that it will become part of education.

That is a false dichotomy.

There is a more defensible middle ground: AI should be integrated into education, but only where it supports human reasoning rather than replaces it.

AI can support learning — but that claim needs a qualifier

The benefits of AI are not imaginary. A student who is confused by a theory can ask for another explanation immediately. Someone revising can generate practice questions or ask for feedback on their understanding. Unlike a lecturer, an AI tool is available at midnight, does not get impatient, and can rephrase an explanation several different ways.

Research supports some of this potential. Kasneci et al. (2023) argue that large language models can support personalised learning, generate educational material and increase opportunities for interaction. These are meaningful advantages, particularly when students need immediate support.

But the word “can” matters. Saying AI can support learning is not the same as saying AI automatically improves learning. That second claim contains an assumption: if technology helps someone complete a task more successfully, they must have learned more. Evidence suggests that assumption is unsafe.

Better work does not always mean better learning

Imagine two students handing in equally polished essays. One has spent hours reading, comparing evidence and deciding which claims are convincing. The other has repeatedly prompted an AI system until it produces a strong argument. The final products may look similar, but the thinking behind them is very different.

Fan et al. (2025) studied 117 university students and found that those receiving ChatGPT support improved their essay scores more than students receiving other forms of support. Yet their knowledge gain and ability to transfer learning were not significantly better. The researchers warned that reliance on generative AI could encourage “metacognitive laziness”: students may obtain a stronger product without engaging as deeply in the self-regulated thinking that helps learning last.

Bastani et al. (2025) provide an even clearer warning. In a field experiment involving nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students, access to a standard GPT-based tool substantially improved performance while students were using it. However, when the tool was later removed, those students performed 17% worse than students who had never had access to it. Importantly, the study also tested an AI tutor designed with safeguards that encouraged learning rather than simply giving answers. The negative effect was largely mitigated.

This does not prove that AI weakens learning in every subject or every university setting; Bastani et al. studied school mathematics, so the conclusion should not be stretched beyond the evidence. What it does show is that improved task performance cannot automatically be treated as improved learning. How the technology is designed, and what cognitive work it replaces, appears to matter.

The strongest argument against AI deserves a serious answer

The strongest case against integrating AI into education is academic integrity. Generative AI can write convincing prose in seconds, imitate academic language and make it difficult to know whether an assessment reflects a student’s own ability. Cotton et al. (2024) identify plagiarism and other forms of academic misconduct as significant challenges for universities.

That concern should not be dismissed. If the purpose of an assessment is to demonstrate a student’s reasoning or knowledge, handing that reasoning over to AI undermines the point of the assessment.

However, the premise that AI can facilitate cheating does not by itself establish the conclusion that AI has no legitimate educational use. To reach that conclusion, we have to add another assumption: that harmful uses of AI cannot be separated from useful ones, or that the harms necessarily outweigh the benefits. That assumption is much harder to defend.

A calculator would be inappropriate in a test designed to assess mental arithmetic, but that does not make calculators educationally useless. The same principle can apply to AI. Some assessments may reasonably ban it because independent performance is exactly what is being measured. Others may allow limited use for brainstorming, feedback or checking understanding. Cotton et al. (2024) similarly argue that universities need proactive policies and clear guidance rather than treating the technology as a problem that will disappear.

So the relevant question is not “AI or no AI?” It is “What is this assessment trying to measure, and does AI help or interfere with that?”

AI enthusiasts need critical thinking too

It would also be a mistake to focus only on weaknesses in the anti-AI argument. A fair judgement requires testing the case we prefer as well.

AI systems can produce inaccurate information and present it with remarkable confidence. Kasneci et al. (2023) highlight concerns around unreliable outputs, bias and the need for fact-checking and human oversight. A polished answer can feel authoritative, but fluency is not evidence of truth.

That creates another reasoning risk. Students may start to treat AI output as something to accept rather than something to evaluate. A useful educational approach would do the opposite. Students should be asking: What evidence supports this? Is the source real? What assumptions are being made? Is another conclusion possible?

If AI encourages those questions, it may strengthen critical thinking. If it removes the need to ask them, it may weaken it.

The better answer is AI literacy, not blanket permission

If banning AI is unrealistic and unrestricted use is risky, the strongest conclusion is not a compromise for compromise’s sake. It is to teach students how to use AI critically and place limits around the situations in which it is appropriate.

Ng et al. (2021) describe AI literacy as involving four broad areas: understanding AI, using it, evaluating it and recognising ethical issues. That is much more demanding than simply knowing how to write a good prompt. A genuinely AI-literate student should know when a chatbot is useful, when its output needs checking, what information should not be shared with it and when using it would defeat the purpose of the task.

There is also a practical reason universities need to engage with this. HEPI’s 2026 survey found that although AI use among students was almost universal, only 36% felt encouraged by their institution to use it (Stephenson & Armstrong, 2026). Students are therefore already making decisions about AI, whether universities guide those decisions or not.

The evidence from Bastani et al. (2025) suggests guardrails can matter. Their learning-focused AI tutor largely avoided the harm associated with unrestricted GPT access. Universities could apply the same principle by designing tasks where AI supports thinking rather than supplies the finished answer: students might use it to generate practice questions, challenge an argument or receive formative feedback, but then have to verify claims, justify their choices and demonstrate independent reasoning.

This still needs a qualifier. Not every assessment should allow AI. Where the purpose is to measure unaided knowledge, reasoning or professional competence, restricting it may be entirely justified. The stronger position is therefore not that AI should always be permitted, but that rules should follow the learning objective rather than a blanket assumption that all AI use is either cheating or progress.

The real question is who is doing the thinking

AI is neither automatically the future of education nor automatically its downfall. Both claims are easier than the evidence.

The more useful question is whether AI extends a student’s thinking or replaces it. If it explains, challenges, scaffolds and gives feedback, it may become a valuable learning tool. If it supplies the reasoning, evidence and judgement, students may produce better-looking work while learning less.

Banning AI will not teach students how to judge it, and unrestricted access will not teach them that either. Universities should instead do what education is supposed to do: help people think critically. In an age of generative AI, that includes knowing when a machine can help, and when it is time to close the chatbot and think for yourself.

Methods

This blog post was developed using the argumentation and critical-thinking techniques introduced in Unit 6. Following Halpern and Dunn’s (2023) approach, the argument was constructed by identifying relevant premises, examining the assumptions connecting those premises to conclusions, and using qualifiers and counterarguments rather than presenting the final judgement as absolute. Halpern and Dunn’s (2023, p. 159) Making Arguments Template also informed the development of the central argument. Alternative explanations and evidence opposing the preferred conclusion were considered to reduce myside bias, consistent with Stanovich and West (2008), who found that individuals may evaluate arguments more favourably when they support their existing views. Toulmin’s (2003) model of argumentation informed the use of claims, evidence, warrants, qualifications and rebuttals. The strength and credibility of competing premises were evaluated rather than treating opinion as fact. In particular, false-dichotomy reasoning was identified where AI is presented as a choice between complete prohibition and unrestricted adoption, while hidden assumptions were examined where examples of AI-assisted cheating are used to conclude that all educational uses are harmful. These techniques supported a qualified judgement that AI should support, rather than replace, students’ reasoning.
