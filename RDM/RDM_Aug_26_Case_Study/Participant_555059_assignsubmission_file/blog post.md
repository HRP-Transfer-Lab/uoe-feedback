Online Learning Isn’t Replacing the Classroom — And It Shouldn’t Have To.

What if the biggest mistake we are making about the future of education is assuming that online learning and the classroom have to compete?

Every time online learning is discussed, the argument seems to become a competition. Online or classroom? Which is better? Which produces better students? And, increasingly, should one eventually replace the other?

I think we are asking the wrong question.

Online learning has changed education, and evidence shows it can work. But traditional classrooms also offer important benefits. So why are we so determined to find a winner?

Perhaps there doesn't need to be one.

This matters because online learning is no longer simply a pandemic response. It has become an established part of education, while face-to-face teaching remains central to schools, colleges and universities. The question is therefore no longer whether online learning will exist, but how we should use it.

After looking closely at the evidence, my position is straightforward: online learning should not replace traditional classroom education, but neither should traditional education automatically be treated as superior.

The better question is when, for whom and under what conditions each approach works best.

Online learning has genuine advantages.

It is easy to see why online learning appeals. It can remove travel, make study more flexible and fit education around employment, caring responsibilities or other commitments.

There is evidence that online learning can produce strong academic outcomes. Khan and Khan (2024), for example, found better listening outcomes among students receiving online English-language teaching in their particular study. Cleland et al. (2022) also found that moving a highly structured team-based learning approach online maintained learning outcomes among medical students.

These findings challenge the assumption that effective learning must happen in a physical classroom. But they do not prove that online learning is universally better. The studies involved particular learners, subjects and teaching designs. A successful online English-language course cannot, by itself, establish that online delivery will produce equivalent outcomes for every subject or learner.

So, the important question is not simply, “Does online learning work?”

It is, “When, for whom and under what conditions does it work?”

Flexibility does not automatically mean better learning.

There is also evidence that online education can create significant difficulties. Walters et al. (2022) studied 407 secondary-school pupils in Wales and found lower reported concentration, engagement and perceived ability to learn during online education than during normal classroom learning. The effects were particularly noticeable among pupils with specific learning difficulties.

The study examined pupils’ experiences during the first UK lockdown, when schools had to move online rapidly. That is very different from a course deliberately designed for online delivery, with appropriate resources, interaction and support.

Emergency remote teaching is not necessarily the same thing as good online teaching. Treating the two as equivalent risks drawing a conclusion that the evidence does not fully support.

There is another issue. The COVID-19 lockdown was an extraordinary and highly emotional period, and for many people it may be their most memorable experience of online education. Halpern and Dunn (2023) explain that memory is not a perfect record: people can be highly confident in their recollections while still remembering events inaccurately, particularly when experiences are emotional. Existing beliefs can also influence what we remember.

That does not make personal experience meaningless. It means we should be careful about turning “this was my experience” into “therefore this is how online learning works”.

What does the bigger picture tell us?

This is where the wider research becomes particularly useful.

A 2026 meta-analysis by Küçük-Avcı and İstanbullu found an overall positive effect of online learning on academic achievement. At first glance, that sounds like an easy win for online education. But the analysis also showed substantial variation.

For me, that variation is just as important as the overall positive result.

If online learning consistently produced the same benefit regardless of subject, age group or educational context, the argument would be much simpler. Instead, the evidence suggests that context matters. The average tells us something useful, but not that every student will learn better online.

There is also an important distinction between demonstrating higher academic achievement and demonstrating better education overall. Grades and test performance do not necessarily capture engagement, wellbeing, social development or the quality of the learning experience.

This is why statistics need to be interpreted rather than simply repeated. A large overall effect can be persuasive, but it can conceal differences between learners and learning environments.

The same caution applies to individual studies. Lin (2022), for example, found that face-to-face students performed better than fully online students in one Statistics for Business comparison, although the performance difference was no longer statistically significant when Zoom lectures were introduced. Face-to-face students reported higher overall satisfaction, but the addition of synchronous online teaching reduced that gap.

That is a particularly useful finding because it shows that online is not one fixed experience. The way technology is used can change the learning experience.

Maybe we don't actually need to choose.

Research into blended learning provides perhaps the strongest challenge to the idea that online and classroom education have to compete.

Müller and Mildenberger (2021), in a systematic review of blended learning in higher education, found broadly equivalent learning outcomes when a substantial proportion of classroom time was replaced with online learning. Their findings suggest that reducing face-to-face teaching does not automatically reduce academic achievement.

Similarly, Selcuk et al. (2025) conducted a multicentre randomised study in clinical pharmacy education and found no significant differences in knowledge gains between students using online simulation and those receiving traditional classroom teaching. Both approaches produced improvements.

Neither study proves that blended or online learning is always better. But that is precisely why they are useful: the real choice may not be online versus classroom at all.

Traditional classrooms clearly have strengths. Face-to-face teaching can provide immediate interaction with lecturers and classmates, social connection and a structured learning environment. Lin (2022) also found higher overall satisfaction among face-to-face students.

Online learning can also provide flexibility and accessibility that a fixed timetable cannot always offer.

Perhaps the sensible answer is to use the right method for the right learning task. A practical skill requiring hands-on supervision may benefit from face-to-face teaching. A lecture-based topic might work perfectly well online. Some students may benefit from classroom discussion followed by online resources they can revisit independently. Others may need the structure and social environment of physically attending a class.

The strongest educational model may therefore be one that treats online and classroom teaching as complementary rather than competing approaches.

We also need to examine our own thinking.

There is another reason this debate becomes so polarised: we are not always as objective as we think we are.

Halpern and Dunn (2023) argue that critical thinking involves more than finding evidence that supports a conclusion. A critical thinker should seek contradictory evidence, consider alternative perspectives and remain willing to revise a conclusion when better evidence becomes available.

That is particularly relevant here. If I begin with the belief that classrooms are obviously better, I can easily find evidence about concentration, interaction and student wellbeing to support my position. If I begin with the belief that online learning is the future, I can point instead to flexibility, accessibility and positive academic outcomes.

Both positions can find evidence.

The harder question is whether we are actually evaluating that evidence fairly.

Stanovich and West (2008) demonstrated that people can show myside, or one-sided, thinking, favouring arguments that support their existing position. Being capable of reasoning does not necessarily protect us from this tendency.

Halpern and Dunn (2023) also emphasise the importance of actively considering alternative viewpoints and being willing to change our minds when evidence changes. That is a useful test for this debate. If the evidence does not support an absolute conclusion, then an absolute conclusion should not be our goal.

Using Toulmin’s approach to argumentation reinforces the same principle. A persuasive claim needs relevant evidence and reasoning, while qualifiers and counterarguments help prevent us from presenting a conclusion as more certain than the evidence allows (THUNK, 2016).

The evidence points towards a more complicated answer.

I would not argue that online learning is bad.

I would not argue that traditional education is always better either.

The evidence does not justify either extreme.

Online learning can produce strong academic outcomes. It can provide flexibility and accessibility. In some carefully structured courses, it can perform as well as, or even better than, face-to-face teaching.

But traditional classrooms continue to offer important benefits, particularly where interaction, structure, practical activity or immediate support are central to learning.

The research itself also has limitations. Individual studies are often based on particular age groups, subjects or institutions. Even meta-analyses combine studies that differ in design and context, and the 2026 analysis reported considerable variation between its included studies. This means we should be wary of turning an average research finding into a universal rule.

So, after weighing the evidence rather than simply choosing the side I prefer, I reach a qualified conclusion:

Neither online nor traditional education is inherently superior. Effective education depends on the relationship between the learner, the learning task and the way teaching is designed.

I don’t want online learning to replace the classroom.

But I don’t want the classroom to replace opportunities that online learning can provide either.

Perhaps the future of education isn’t about choosing a winner.

Perhaps it is about being smart enough to use both.

Methods

The argument was developed using Halpern and Dunn’s (2023) framework for analysing arguments, with particular attention to the identification of premises, conclusions, assumptions, supporting evidence and counterarguments. The online-versus-traditional education debate was analysed for the false dichotomy of presenting the two approaches as mutually exclusive, with blended learning considered as an alternative position. The analysis also considered card stacking or suppressed information, examining whether evidence supporting one educational approach could be emphasised while relevant evidence supporting the alternative was omitted. This supported a more balanced evaluation of the available evidence. Toulmin’s method was applied by establishing a central claim supported by evidence and reasoning, while incorporating qualifiers and counterarguments to avoid overstating the conclusion (THUNK, 2016). The potential influence of myside and one-sided thinking was also considered, drawing on Stanovich and West (2008), by evaluating evidence supporting both online and face-to-face education. Findings from empirical studies and systematic reviews were compared across different educational contexts, learner groups, subjects and study designs, with methodological limitations and the distinction between emergency remote teaching and intentionally designed online education considered when evaluating the strength and generalisability of conclusions. Overall, the approach sought to develop a qualified judgement based on the relevance, balance and limitations of the evidence rather than selecting evidence solely because it supported a predetermined conclusion.
