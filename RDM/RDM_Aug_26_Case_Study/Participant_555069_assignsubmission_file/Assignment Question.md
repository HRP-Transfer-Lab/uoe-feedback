Should we all go vegetarian or just eat less meat?

When you walk into a supermarket, it is difficult to miss how prominently meat is displayed. Entire sections are dedicated to meat products, while vegetarian and vegan options most of the time occupy a much smaller section, or they are spread across the store. This raises the question of whether some of us eat meat because it is widely accessible or visible. For many people, food is much more than just fuel for the body. Meat can be connected to people’s cultures, lifestyle, personal choices, and economic interests. At the same time, vegetarianism has become increasingly popular for health and environmental benefits, and concerns for animal welfare.

There are convincing arguments for both sides. Regardless of the amount of evidence, neither side is right or wrong; it is important to consider evidence from both diets.  Raising the question of whether the solution is to reduce the amount of consumption of meat rather than cutting it out altogether.

Health impacts

Following a vegetarian diet can provide many health benefits if done correctly. The American Dietetic Association states that a healthy, balanced vegetarian or vegan diet can reduce the chance of developing certain diseases (Jedut et al., 2023). Furthermore, vegetarian diets can reduce the chances of developing cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, dementia, and cancer (Wang et al., 2023). A meta-analysis study involving 13 studies and 844,157 participants found that vegetarian diets reduced cardiovascular diseases by 15% and reduced ischemic heart disease by 21% (Jedut et al., 2023). Jedut et al. (2023), found that following an omnivore diet can result in a 30% mortality risk due to ischemic disease. According to statistic diabetes was 46% less common in plant-based diets than in omnivores.

However, this evidence does not automatically mean that every vegetarian meal is healthy. A diet can be meat-free and still be dominated by refined carbohydrates, high-fructose and saturated fats. A healthy balanced vegetarian diet includes complex fibre-rich carbohydrates, vitamins, phytochemicals, MUFA and PUFA, potassium, magnesium and selenium (Wang et al., 2023). Some individuals following a vegetarian diet can experience nutritional deficiencies such as protein, omega-3, vitamin D, iron, B12, calcium, and zinc (Jedut et al., 2023). This is because vegetarian and vegan alternatives are highly ultra-processed and packed with oils and saturated fats.

Therefore, the evidence does not suggest that removing meat automatically equals a healthier diet. Instead, it suggests that a well-planned vegetarian diet can offer many health benefits and reduce the dangers of certain diseases associated with eating meat religiously.

Environmental impacts

Furthermore, one of the main reasons people should consider cutting down their meat consumption is for the environment. Animal agriculture is leading to habitat destruction; cattle farming is responsible for 71% of Latin American deforestation, resulting in an annual loss of 2.71 million hectares (Rust et al., 2020). In addition, cattle produce a chemical called methane, which has a global warming impact 27 times greater than carbon dioxide in the past 100 years (Kass, 2024). These statistics are scary, and there are growing concerns for our environment, as in 2024 there were approximately 1.58 billion cattle worldwide (Kass, 2024).

On-farm sources of greenhouse gases made up 82% of total GWP for dairy production and 90% for beef production; methane was the biggest contributor at 62% for dairy and 73% for beef (O’Brien et al., 2023). O’Brien et al. (2023) found that organic farming reduced environmental impacts per hectare but reduced productivity significantly. Scarborough et al. (2023) found that vegans, vegetarians and fish eaters have less of an impact on the environment than meat eaters.

It is known that meat, specifically beef, is having catastrophic effects on the environment; however, does that mean going vegetarian is not bad for the environment also? There is no comparison that meat production is worse for the planet, but there are some fruits and vegetables which also have significant impacts on carbon emissions.

For example, potatoes are one of the biggest contributors to the environmental impact of the UK sector; they account for 57-59% of total greenhouse gas emissions (Frankowska et al., 2019). The high percentage is mostly due to crisps and chips and the fact that they require 8 months of cold storage at specific temperatures (Frankowska et al., 2019). Other foods that have significant impacts include beans, tomatoes, carrots, and peas. (Frankowska et al., 2019). Therefore, before cutting out meat for environmental reasons, also consider the fruits and vegetables that are having major impacts on the planet’s health.

Slaughterhouses and animal welfare

One of the strongest arguments for vegetarianism is the question of animal welfare. Many slaughterhouse conditions raise concerns about whether the animals are being treated with respect, and many in the general population are not actually aware of how bad it is. Furthermore, during the slaughter process, there is a risk that some larger animals like cattle, pigs, and sheep may not be stunned effectively (RSPCA, 2026). Leading to animals regaining consciousness and suffering unnecessary pain and suffering (RSPCA, 2026).

Moving on, poultry face their own welfare concerns. Similarly, in some slaughter conditions, birds are shackled while they are still conscious, and then electric currents are passed through their body via water (RSPCA, 2026). However, sometimes electrical currents are not strong enough to ensure unconsciousness, and therefore create a risk of them missing the neck cutter (RSPCA, 2026). This can result in the possibility of them still being alive when entering the scalding tank, which is required to loosen feathers before plucking (RSPCA, 2026).

These practices raise significant ethical concerns about meat production and the lack of awareness of the suffering animals are going through during these processes. However, it would be unfair to conclude that every farmer or slaughterhouse operates under the same welfare standards. Consumers of meat can carefully select meat from farming systems they support, which will reduce the consumption of purchasing meat from lower-welfare producers.

Economic considerations

Leading on, being a vegetarian is often cheaper and more ethical than consuming meat. Animal-based foods such as meat and fish are more expensive than plant-based protein sources (López-Moreno et al., 2026). López-Moreno et al. (2026) conducted a study which found that a Mediterranean diet significantly reduced not only negative environmental impact but also the overall cost of food; this was a result of replacing animal-based products like meat and fish with high-fibre plant-based options.

Research suggests that adopting a vegetarian diet or just cutting back on meat can reduce your food bill by a third (University of Oxford, 2021). Dr Marco Springman found that, in high-income countries, vegan diets were the most affordable, with vegetarian diets a close second, and that flexitarian diets reduced food costs by 14% (University of Oxford, 2021).

However, what impact would everyone going vegetarian have on livestock farms and food systems? Yiorgos Vittis et al. (2025) explored how flexitarian, pescatarian, vegetarian, and vegan diets can affect the number of people working in the food industry in 179 countries.

They found that adopting more plant-based diets will reduce global agricultural labour by 5-28% by 2030; overall, these changes would reduce labour costs by US $290- 995 per year (Yiorgos Vittis et al., 2025).

Cultural consideration

Vegetarianism can be difficult for some individuals to conform to due to cultural challenges, as food is very much a part of many traditions, cultures and social relationships. It can be argued that eating meat is culturally and socially embedded; adopting a vegetarian diet is not just a nutritional decision. Vegetarians share a set of beliefs about the consumption of meat, whether that is for health reasons, economic reasons, or animal welfare (Nezlek & Forestell, 2020). Furthermore, going vegetarian can shape somebody’s attitudes, beliefs, and well-being (Nezlek & Forestell, 2020). Research found that vegetarians tend to be more neurotic, depressed and anxious; vegetarians also reported lower self-esteem, psychological adjustment, and more negative moods in comparison to omnivores (Nezlek & Forestell, 2020).

Reddy & van Dam (2020) found that food serves as an important way to communicate ethnic identities; each cultural group uses specific cooking techniques and foods to distinguish themselves from others. Research was conducted across 130 Chinese, Indian, and Malay women, and three themes were found across the results: food enables identity construction, cross-cultural adoption is common, use of traditional foods as medicine (Reddy & van Dam, 2020). This demonstrates that vegetarianism is more complicated than cutting meat for health and environmental reasons; it can deeply affect your social life.

Ultimately, the vegetarianism vs omnivores debate is not just about what is on our plates. It is a lot more complex than that; it involves health, environment, animal suffering, culture, economics, and personal freedom. Vegetarianism presents a strong case for reducing climate change and the ethical costs of meat production; however, omnivores have cultural and economic arguments. In my opinion, the most responsible approach would be to reduce the amount of meat consumption and make better choices when selecting meat. For example, researching the farms that your meat is coming from and selecting farms which have a higher animal-welfare standard than others.

Methods

Critical thinking was used throughout this blog post to evaluate whether reducing meat consumption or adopting a vegetarian diet is the most appropriate approach. The arguments were considered by using multiple perspectives and viewpoints. An open-minded approach was used throughout to avoid a one-sided bias. Furthermore, evidence was assessed by using careful consideration of the relevance and credibility of studies, statistics, and sources before using them to support claims. Critical thinking techniques were used to counterbalance many points made. For example, the removal of meat entirely can be argued to be healthier or more environmentally friendly; however, there are also many negative health and environmental impacts of following a vegetarian lifestyle. These counterarguments were used alongside many points to reduce confirmation bias and provide a more balanced discussion. Many points were also covered to develop a strong argument on the complexity of the vegetarian vs omnivore debate; these included: economic aspects, environmental impacts, health impacts, cultural and social impacts, and welfare and ethics of slaughterhouses. Deductive reasoning was also used to assess whether conclusions logically followed evidence. Finally, as these arguments were weighed against each other, the conclusion was that the best approach is to eat less meat rather than cutting it out entirely, reflecting a hybrid approach to this debate. Following Halpern & Dunn (2022), the aim was not simply to collect information, but to use critical thinking skills to make a balanced and reasonable argument by looking at both sides of an argument.
