AI Belongs in the Classroom – But It Should Never Replace the Teacher

Whether education systems are ready for it or not, artificial intelligence (AI) has arrived in full swing.

Students can ask a chatbot to simplify difficult concepts, generate practice questions, provide feedback on an essay or produce an answer in seconds. Teachers can use similar tools to create lesson plans, develop activities and organise information. AI has already affected education; it is no longer a question or debate.  The more important question is this: should we embrace AI in education, or protect education from it?

The answer? AI should be integrated into education, but carefully and deliberately. Proper use can make learning more accessible and support teachers. Poor use can encourage students to avoid thinking and make it easier to produce an answer without actually learning (Miao & Holmes, 2023; OECD, 2026).  The future of education should not be “AI versus teachers”. It should be teachers using AI to help students become better thinkers and learners.

AI could make learning more personal

One of the strongest arguments for AI in education is its ability to provide immediate and sometimes, personalised support. Research increasingly suggests that generative AI can improve some learning outcomes when it is used as part of educational activities rather than as a tool for generating answers (Chen & Cheung, 2025; Deng et al., 2025).

A teacher could have thirty students in a classroom, all with different learning needs. Even the best teacher cannot provide every student with individual feedback at every moment in a day. AI can potentially fill some of these gaps by allowing students to ask questions, ask for explanations or practise a skill at their own pace.

The OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026 identifies several promising uses of AI, including its potential to act as a tutor and assistant when it is guided by appropriate teaching principles (OECD, 2026). AI is not automatically educational just because it can produce an impressive answer.

Imagine a student struggling to understand photosynthesis. They could ask an AI chatbot to explain it in simpler terms and produce practice questions to strengthen their understanding. Used in this way, AI could become another learning resource rather than a shortcut.

There is an important difference between using AI to think better and using AI instead of thinking.  That distinction should shape how schools and universities approach using AI tools.

AI could help teachers

The debate about AI often focuses entirely on students. But teachers may benefit as well.

Teachers spend significant time on tasks that are important but not crucial. AI can potentially help with lesson planning, generating examples, creating practice materials and organising information (OECD, 2026).

The OECD (2026) reports that 37% of lower-secondary teachers surveyed in 2024 had used AI for their work, while 57% agreed that AI could help write or improve lesson plans. These numbers suggest that AI is already becoming part of teachers' lives.  But “AI can help teachers” should not become “AI can replace teachers.”

A good teacher doesn’t than just deliver information. Teachers notice when a student is confused, they can encourage less confident students and make professional judgements about what individual students need.

UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education advocates a human-centred approach where AI augments human capabilities while protecting human agency and the rights of learners (Miao & Holmes, 2023).  That seems like a sensible idea. We should use technology to remove unnecessary burdens from teachers, allowing more time for teaching, mentoring and interacting with students.

There is a problem: AI can make students look smarter without making them smarter

This is where the excitement surrounding AI needs to be discussed.

An AI chatbot can write an essay that looks sophisticated, solve mathematical problems and produce convincing explanations. But if a student were to copy and paste, where is the learning?  The OECD (2026) identifies an important difference between performing a task successfully and learning from the task. Evidence suggests that general-purpose AI can improve the quality of students' outputs without necessarily improving their learning overall. When cognitive work is simply passed to a chatbot, students can become less engaged with the thinking needed to develop knowledge and skills.  This concern is consistent with experimental and review evidence. Deng et al. (2025) found that ChatGPT interventions can improve academic performance and some higher-order-thinking outcomes, but also identified the need to distinguish the quality of AI-generated outputs from genuine learning and recommended assessments that require students to demonstrate their learned skills. Chen and Cheung (2025) also found positive effects across several university learning outcomes, while finding no significant effect on metacognition. These findings suggest that the educational value of AI depends heavily on how it is used.  This is worrying because education is not simply about producing correct answers, it is about learning the solutions to problems.

Writing an essay requires an individual to select evidence, organise ideas, evaluate arguments and make a conclusion. This is all part of the learning process. If AI performs all of them, the student may receive a better essay but develop weaker skills.  This creates a paradox: AI could make students' work look better while making students' thinking worse.

That is why banning AI altogether is not necessarily the answer. Instead, education needs to change how AI is used and how learning is assessed.  Students should be taught to question AI-generated information, identify weaknesses in arguments, verify evidence and explain their own reasoning. An AI-generated answer should be the beginning of critical thinking.

Privacy is another reason to be cautious

There is also another problem: data.

Students may enter personal information or sensitive questions into AI chatbots without understanding what happens to that information after they press send. UNESCO's guidance warns that generative AI raises concerns about data privacy, safety and the protection of students, particularly where regulatory frameworks and institutional safeguards have not kept pace with the technology (Miao & Holmes, 2023).  This matters particularly when children and young people are involved.

Schools have a responsibility to know what technologies they are introducing, what information those technologies collect and whether the educational benefits outweigh the risks. AI should not be used simply because it is easy or because other institutions are using it.  UNESCO recommends ethical validation and consideration of whether AI systems are educationally appropriate, safe and suitable for particular students (Miao & Holmes, 2023). The OECD (2026) similarly emphasises the importance of privacy, safety, bias testing, age-appropriateness and transparency in developing trustworthy uses of AI in education.  “Can we use this technology?” is the wrong first question.  The better question is: “Should we use this technology for this particular educational purpose?”

We should stop treating this as an all-or-nothing debate

Much of the conversation about AI falls into two categories.

One side sees AI as an educational revolution that will improve learning. The other sees it as a threat to academic integrity and the future of genuine education.  Both positions contain some truth.  AI could improve access to educational support, helping teachers and providing students with new ways to practise and understand ideas. Research reviews have found evidence of positive effects on academic performance and some higher-order-thinking outcomes, although the findings also show limitations and variation depending on how AI is integrated into learning (Chen & Cheung, 2025; Deng et al., 2025). But it can also aid plagiarism, produce false information and encourage students to disengage their brains.

Assuming that AI improves or destroys learning exclusively would be a mistake.  The more reasonable position is a conditional one: AI is valuable when it strengthens learning, but it is harmful when it replaces the cognitive thinking that education should develop.  This means educational institutions should establish clear rules rather than just banning or adopting AI.

Students need AI literacy: they should understand what AI can and cannot do. They should learn how to check its statements against reliable evidence and recognise that impressive doesn’t always mean correct.  Teachers need training too. Institutions should provide clear guidance about appropriate uses of AI and protect student privacy.

Assessment may also need to change. If an assessment can be completed convincingly by AI, maybe the assessment needs to measure more than just the end result. Oral discussion, reflection and practical tasks can all give students the opportunity to show that they actually understand their work. This approach is consistent with research recommending more complex, skill-demonstration-based assessment when evaluating learning in an environment using AI (Deng et al., 2025).

Most importantly, humans must remain responsible for important educational decisions.

The goal should be better thinkers, not better-looking answers

Education has always changed alongside technology. Calculators did not make mathematics disappear. Search engines did not make teachers irrelevant. Word processors did not make writing meaningless.  But every technological development changes what skills students need.  AI is different in scale, but the principle stays the same. We should not ask whether technology can perform a task, but ask whether allowing technology to perform that task helps or inhibits the student long term.  The answer could be yes or no.  AI can explain, generate, suggest and challenge. It can even tutor.  But it cannot take responsibility for a student's education.

The future I want to see is therefore not a classroom where every student works alone with a chatbot, it is also not a classroom where technology is forbidden.  It is a classroom where students use AI to help them, not do the work for them. Teachers must stay in control, and there should be more emphasis on critical thinking.  The greatest danger of artificial intelligence in education is not that machines will become too intelligent, rather that we might become comfortable with not thinking for ourselves.

If education is to prepare students to question and evaluate, then these abilities have to stay at the centre. AI should help students develop critical thinking, not do the thinking for them.

Methods

This blog post used multiple critical thinking concepts and techniques to evaluate the role of AI in education. Firstly, argument analysis was used by considering both the benefits and risks of AI rather than adopting a solely positive or negative opinion. I compared different ideas and evaluated if the available evidence supported them or not, when discussing AI’s effects on learning and academic performance (Chen & Cheung, 2025; Deng et al., 2025). I also used evidence-based reasoning by supporting key arguments with research from systematic reviews and reports from organisations such as UNESCO and the OECD. Also, I applied questioning and evaluation, particularly by arguing ideas that technology is automatically beneficial simply because it produces impressive results. This can be seen in the distinction made between successfully completing a task and genuinely learning from it. These methods helped me reach a balanced conclusion based on evaluating evidence, taking into account different perspectives and recognising the limitations of both extreme positions.
