AI Should Be in Education — But It Should Never Do the Thinking for Us

When ChatGPT can explain a difficult concept, generate an essay plan, suggest arguments and rewrite a paragraph in seconds, it is tempting to ask whether students should be allowed to use it at all.

That is the wrong question.

The more important question is: what cognitive work should remain with the student?

My position is a hybrid one. Artificial intelligence should be integrated into education, but only when it strengthens learning rather than replacing the thinking that learning is supposed to develop. The educational value of AI depends less on what the technology can produce than on what the student is still required to do.

There is good reason to be optimistic. Recent evidence suggests that generative AI can improve learning outcomes. Chen and Cheung's (2025) meta-analysis of 57 studies found a large overall effect of generative AI on university students' learning outcomes (g = 0.804), including a moderate effect on higher-order thinking (g = 0.580). Another meta-analysis of 68 experimental and quasi-experimental studies found a moderate overall effect (SMD = 0.45) (Han et al., 2025). These findings make it difficult to defend the claim that AI is inherently educationally harmful.

However, effect sizes are not the same as proof that AI improves learning in every context. Han et al. (2025) reported extremely high heterogeneity between studies (I² = 95%), while stronger effects were associated with factors such as shorter interventions and smaller samples. Chen and Cheung (2025) also found no statistically significant improvement in metacognition. This matters because performing better on an assessment is not necessarily equivalent to becoming a better independent thinker. If students achieve a higher score because AI supplied part of the reasoning, we need to ask whether the student has actually developed the underlying skill.

There is also a methodological caution. A meta-analysis can produce a persuasive average while concealing very different interventions, subjects and learner groups. With I² at 95% in Han et al. (2025), the average benefit cannot sensibly be treated as a universal effect. If larger gains are concentrated in short, small-sample studies, novelty, researcher support or unusually controlled conditions may partly explain them. The evidence therefore supports the narrower claim that AI can improve learning under some conditions; it does not yet establish that those gains will transfer to long-term, everyday independent study.

This is where the debate becomes more interesting.

Critical thinking is not simply producing a correct answer. It involves purposeful cognitive skills for analysing information, evaluating evidence, making inferences and deciding what to believe or do (Halpern & Dunn, 2023). If a student asks AI to generate the explanation, identify the strongest evidence, evaluate opposing arguments and produce the conclusion, the final answer may look impressive while the student has practised relatively little of the thinking involved.

This is the danger of cognitive offloading. We already use external tools to reduce cognitive demands; the problem is not offloading itself, but offloading processes that education is intended to develop. Emerging research suggests that this distinction matters. Li et al. (2026), in a systematic review of 67 empirical studies, found that ChatGPT can support critical and creative thinking when embedded within inquiry-oriented and scaffolded learning. In less structured contexts, however, studies also reported cognitive offloading and reductions in critical engagement.

A separate systematic review of 65 high-quality empirical studies reached a similarly nuanced conclusion. Guo et al. (2026) found that ChatGPT can enhance analytical, interpretive and reasoning skills when appropriately integrated, but found no consistent evidence that it develops deeper critical-thinking dispositions such as truth-seeking and systematicity. In other words, students may become better at performing some critical-thinking tasks without necessarily developing the disposition to question information independently.

So perhaps the real problem is not AI.

It is how we use it.

Imagine two students receive the same essay question. Student A asks ChatGPT to write the essay and submits a polished response. Student B asks it to challenge their argument, identify assumptions, generate the strongest counterargument and point out evidence that would weaken their conclusion. Both have used AI. Only one has clearly used it to stimulate reasoning.

That distinction suggests a better model for AI integration: make the student the reasoner and AI the intellectual sparring partner.

For example, students could be required to formulate an initial position before consulting AI, explain why they reached it, ask AI to generate the strongest opposing argument, verify its factual claims against independent sources and then revise their position. This turns AI from an answer-generating shortcut into a tool for hypothesis testing, evaluation and metacognitive monitoring. It also reflects Halpern and Dunn's (2023) emphasis on deliberate critical-thinking skills rather than simply possessing information.

AI could also be particularly valuable for personalised learning. It can provide immediate explanations, alternative examples and feedback at a pace suited to the learner. However, personalisation should not be confused with effectiveness. A system can make learning feel easier without necessarily producing durable learning. Indeed, Chen and Cheung's (2025) finding that metacognition did not significantly improve should make educators cautious about assuming that a more responsive learning experience automatically produces more independent learners.

There is another problem: AI can sound convincing when it is wrong.

Students who assume that an apparently confident answer is probably accurate risk automation bias, over-reliance on an automated recommendation rather than independently evaluating the information (Goddard et al., 2012). This is particularly concerning in education because students are still developing expertise. A novice may be less able than an expert to recognise when an AI-generated explanation contains a subtle error. Research on automation bias has long shown that decision-support systems can reduce vigilance in information processing when users place too much trust in automated recommendations.

This means AI literacy cannot simply mean knowing how to write effective prompts. Students also need to learn how to interrogate AI outputs:

What evidence supports this claim? What might be missing? Does another source disagree? What assumptions has the answer made? What would convince me that it is wrong?

These questions are critical because AI can amplify existing cognitive biases rather than eliminate them. A student who already believes a particular conclusion may ask AI for supporting evidence and then accept a plausible-looking response that confirms the original belief. Critical thinking therefore requires not only access to information, but a willingness to consider information that could challenge our preferred conclusion.

The same principle applies to academic integrity. Banning AI may appear straightforward, but prohibition does not resolve the underlying educational problem. Students will still encounter AI beyond university, where the ability to evaluate, challenge and work alongside these systems is likely to matter. UNESCO (2023) therefore advocates a human-centred approach combining the possibilities of generative AI with safeguards around privacy, ethics, equity and meaningful use.

The challenge is to regulate AI without turning education into a technological arms race between students and detection software.

A more useful response is to make thinking visible in assessment. Rather than rewarding only a polished final product, educators could require students to show how they evaluated sources, challenged an AI response, justified revisions and explained why they rejected alternative claims. This would also make plagiarism a less useful shortcut because the assessed skill would be the reasoning process, not merely the prose AI can generate.

This brings us back to the central issue: what is education for?

If education is primarily about producing polished outputs, then letting AI produce those outputs makes considerable sense. But if education is supposed to develop people who can reason, evaluate evidence, solve problems and make informed decisions, replacing those cognitive processes with AI is self-defeating.

The answer is therefore neither “AI everywhere” nor “AI nowhere.”

It is purposeful integration.

Using Toulmin's (2003) model of argument, the claim is that AI should be integrated into education. The grounds are the growing experimental evidence of improved learning outcomes. The warrant is that AI can provide feedback, explanation, personalised support and opportunities for inquiry. The qualifier is crucial: these benefits depend on how AI is embedded within learning. The rebuttal is that unrestricted use can encourage cognitive offloading, automation bias and superficial engagement.

Importantly, this conclusion should remain open to revision. My position would change if high-quality longitudinal research consistently showed that carefully scaffolded AI use weakened students' independent reasoning, metacognition or ability to evaluate evidence. Equally, claims that AI is transformative should be treated cautiously until effects are demonstrated across different subjects, educational levels and longer periods rather than predominantly short-term interventions. The current evidence is promising, but its substantial heterogeneity means that “AI works” is too simplistic a conclusion.

AI does belong in education.

But the goal should not be to make students better at getting answers from machines. It should be to make them better at deciding whether those answers deserve to be believed.

That is the difference between using AI to make learning easier and using it to make learning better.

Methods

This argument was developed using Halpern and Dunn's (2023) framework for critical thinking, particularly the deliberate evaluation of evidence, consideration of alternative explanations, hypothesis testing and metacognitive monitoring. Toulmin's (2003) model was used to structure the argument by distinguishing the central claim, supporting grounds, warrant, qualifier and rebuttal. Evidence was evaluated rather than treated as uniformly conclusive: effect sizes, heterogeneity, intervention duration and the distinction between learning outcomes and metacognition were considered when assessing recent meta-analytic findings. The argument also considers cognitive biases, particularly automation bias and the possibility of confirmation bias, because students may accept or selectively seek AI-generated information that supports existing beliefs. Cognitive offloading was considered as an alternative explanation for apparent improvements in performance: AI may improve the product of a student's work without producing equivalent development in the underlying cognitive skill. Finally, the conclusion was deliberately qualified by identifying evidence that could change the position, reflecting open-mindedness and the principle that strong critical thinking requires conclusions to remain proportionate to the available evidence (Halpern & Dunn, 2023).
