Online Learning is not replacing classroom learning, but maybe we should stop asking it to.

For years, education has been guided by a singular, straightforward concept. Students entered the classroom, sat at their desks, listened to the teacher or lecturer, raised their hand if they had a question, and chatted with those sitting next to them. This is what education looked like.

Then online learning changed the educational landscape.

Students could suddenly attend lectures from the comfort of their own homes, connect with peers on a laptop screen, and balance study around jobs and family commitments without travelling to school or campus. However, despite the benefits of online learning, it is sometimes seen as inferior to traditional face-to-face learning.

However, is education more effective when sitting in a classroom?

The debate is often presented as a choice between online learning and traditional classroom education, as if one must defeat the other. However, the evidence suggests we should not view it that way. Online learning should not be seen as replacing traditional education, but it should not be treated as second best either. Instead, we should look at how each approach, or a combination of approaches, works best for the student.

Online learning can work.

Online learning offers flexibility by allowing students to balance education with work, childcare, and personal commitments, eliminating the need to plan life around rigid schedules. The flexibility can benefit those who would otherwise face barriers to learning in a traditional environment by making online learning more accessible. However, flexibility alone is not enough to prove online learning is effective.

Means et al. (2013) compared online and face-to-face learning and found that, on average, students in online learning conditions performed modestly better than those receiving face-to-face teaching. Researchers also found a strong advantage in combining online and face-to-face learning. They cautioned that factors such as additional learning time and instructional resources could have contributed to the results.

Martin et al. (2022) examined previous studies and found a small but statistically significant overall advantage for distance learning compared to face-to-face education across cognitive, behavioural, and affective outcomes.

At a glance, it would be tempting to conclude that online learning wins.

However, this would be an overgeneralisation because the research does not suggest that moving every session to online learning will automatically improve learning. Earlier meta-analysis of 232 studies found that there was no overall difference between distance and classroom education, with considerable variation between individual studies (Bernard et al.,2004)

This distinction matters because good critical thinking requires us to consider alternative explanations rather than simply choosing evidence that supports the conclusion we want.

The classroom does have advantages we should not overlook

Students have valid reasons to prefer traditional education.

Face-to-face teaching provides opportunities for natural discussion and immediate interaction with lecturers and classmates. Students form social connections, become part of real-time academic communities, and gain a sense of daily routine; ignoring these benefits to support online learning creates confirmation bias by focusing only on data that fits the preferred narrative and risks overlooking evidence that tells a different story. Martin et al. (2022) found that online learning offered a slight overall advantage, although the outcomes varied across different contexts. Similarly, Means et al. (2013) found that the advantage was significant for blended learning but not when purely online learning was compared with purely face-to-face learning.

So, claiming that online education is superior would go beyond what the evidence tells us.

Challenging the assumptions of online vs traditional education

Part of the debate comes down to which is better, but shouldn't it be that if a student understands and connects with the material, remembers it, and applies it, it does not matter whether they learned it in a lecture theatre or at their dining table?

The appeal of tradition must be avoided when defending face-to-face learning. Just because traditional teaching is familiar and has worked for a long time does not mean that it is better. The fact that education has always been delivered a certain way does not prove it will always be the best approach.

Another weakness in the debate over online vs traditional education is the false dichotomy, since the choice does not have to be one or the other.

 Blended learning integrates online technology with traditional classroom teaching. This hybrid model lets students benefit from digital flexibility while keeping face-to-face discussion, support, and interaction. Bernard et al. (2014) examined blended learning and technology use in higher education. They found evidence supporting the effectiveness of blended approaches, with some online learning outperforming classroom teaching, while some performed worse. Means et al. (2013) similarly found particularly positive outcomes when online and face-to-face learning were combined.

This does not prove that blended learning will work better for every student, course or institution. This shows that education does not have to choose one option; it can find a middle ground.

Online learning is not one experience.

One of the biggest issues when asking whether online learning works is that online education can mean different things.

Online and face-to-face learning offer distinct experiences. One student watches recorded lectures in isolation, while another actively participates in live seminars, engages in discussions, collaborates on projects, and receives direct feedback. However, sitting in a lecture theatre does not mean that a student is engaged or learning effectively. Careful course design and teaching quality matter far more than the learning location, so researchers should be cautious when generalising findings. Gherghel et al. (2023) support this difference, finding that social interaction during online classes was associated with greater emotional and behavioural engagement among university students. Engagement also helped explain increased self-directed study outside the classroom. This suggests that the effectiveness of online learning may depend partly on how opportunities for interaction are built into the learning experience, rather than simply whether teaching takes place online or face-to-face.

 If one online course produces poor outcomes, it would be hasty to conclude that online education itself does not work. Equally, one successful online programme cannot demonstrate that every course should move online.

Should we stop trying to choose a winner?

So the question is, should classrooms be closed and everything moved online?

No.

However, can we assume that students in classrooms receive a better education?

The evidence does not support this conclusion either.

Both approaches to learning have distinct strengths. Online learning offers flexibility and accessibility, which makes it an excellent choice for students balancing work commitments and family life. On the other hand, traditional education provides structure, social connections, and face-to-face interaction for students who thrive in a classroom environment. Blended learning combines these advantages.

One important point is that no single approach suits every student or subject. Perhaps the question is which approach gives students in a given subject the best opportunity to learn, rather than whether online education is better than traditional classroom education.

The future of education does not need one winner; both approaches matter to learning.

The most strategic approach is knowing when to use each one.

Methods

Several critical thinking concepts, theories and techniques were used to develop this argument. Halpern’s (2014) approach to critical thinking was applied by identifying the central issue, evaluating the quality of supporting evidence, considering alternative explanations and avoiding conclusions that go beyond the available evidence. Argument analysis is important, as the blog examined claims about the superiority of either online or traditional education against the evidence supporting them. Cognitive biases and reasoning fallacies were also considered. The blog addressed confirmation bias by including evidence and arguments that challenged the position. At the same time, the blog identified false dichotomous thinking in the assumption that education must choose between online and traditional approaches. The blog also considered appeal to tradition and hasty generalisation when evaluating claims about educational effectiveness.

The argument used Toulmin's (2003) model, structuring it around a claim, supporting grounds, warrants that connect the evidence to the claim, qualifiers that acknowledge the evidence's limitations, and rebuttals that address opposing arguments. The approach allowed the conclusion to remain conditional rather than absolute and reflects Halpern’s emphasis on evaluating evidence and reaching reasoned conclusions rather than accepting without sufficient evidence (Halpern, 2014)
