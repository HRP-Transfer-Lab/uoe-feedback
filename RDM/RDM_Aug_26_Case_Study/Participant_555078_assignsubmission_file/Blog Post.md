Eating Less Meat Doesn't Have to Be All or Nothing:
Why Our Diet Debate Needs More Middle Ground

HuffPost-style Blog Assignment

Food is never just food. It is culture, heritage, convenience, tradition, lifestyle and, increasingly, identity. Discuss diets, and the conversation can quickly become deeply personal. People will choose vegetarian options, and others will argue passionately about the merits of meat. Vegetarian versus omnivore may come to sound less like a conversation about lunch and more like taking sides in a conflict. I think this is when we get it wrong.

We tend to present the issue of meat consumption as a black-and-white question: it is either essential for a healthy diet, or we must reduce it drastically and become vegetarian. While looking into the existing literature on the issue, however, I have found no evidence to support either of these claims. My conclusion is that reducing meat consumption considerably and adding more plant-based food to our meals could be beneficial, which does not imply ignoring the problems that a vegetarian diet may bring and claiming that everybody must stop eating meat tomorrow. One of the strongest arguments in favor of a vegetarian diet concerns the cardiovascular health benefits of the latter.

According to Dybvik et al. (2023), a meta-analysis and systematic review based on 13 prospective cohort studies involving more than 844,000 participants revealed that a vegetarian and vegan dietary pattern was associated with the decreased risk of ischemic heart disease; some of the other outcomes remained uncertain. The data from the intervention studies are also promising. Wang et al. (2023) have reviewed 20 randomised controlled trials involving people with, or at high risk of, cardiovascular disease. As compared to the control diet, a vegetarian dietary pattern has been associated with lower levels of LDL cholesterol, HbA1c (HbA1c, a measure of longer term blood glucose control) and body weight. But critical thinking requires asking yet another question: what is being compared exactly? A vegetarian diet comprising of vegetables, lentils, beans, whole grains, nuts and fruit is not the same as a diet with a high intake of chips, refined carbohydrates, sweets and highly processed meat substitutes. The lack of meat itself does not mean that the diet is healthy.

As the NHS emphasizes, a healthy vegetarian diet can provide appropriate nutrition for the person but also requires paying special attention to some nutrients such as iron and vitamin B12. Plant-derived iron is harder to absorb than obtained through meat, and vitamin B12 requires additional attention, especially when animal products are limited heavily (NHS, n.d.).

The relevant question, therefore, is not, "Is vegetarian food healthier than meat?" It is: "What are people actually eating instead?" This clarification is needed to avoid yet another reasoning mistake: the reduction of a complex health outcome to one variable. Human beings consume nutrients in a combination and being an "omnivore" or a "vegetarian" does not define the overall quality of their diet.

Then there is the issue of the environment. Here is when the argument for reduced meat consumption starts to make sense to me. According to Scarborough et al. (2023), researchers have investigated dietary data of more than 55,000 people in the UK along with environmental data covering thousands of farms across 119 countries. Environmental impact increased along with the consumption of animal-based foods. High-meat diets had much higher environmental impacts than low-meat, vegetarian and vegan diets in terms of greenhouse-gas emissions, land use and biodiversity. Most importantly, the researchers have recognized an aspect that tends to disappear from social-media debates about food. The place of production and the ways of food production are essential factors. Regardless of this difference, however, the correlation between animal-based food consumption and the level of environmental impact has remained apparent (Scarborough et al., 2023). This does not mean that imported avocado becomes environmentally safe just because it is vegan. It also does not mean that all locally produced animal-based foods have the same impact. It means that when we consider dietary patterns, rather than specific examples, reduced animal-based products consumption brings a meaningful environmental benefit. But telling everyone “To simply go vegetarian" ignores reality. And here is when vegetarian arguments start losing me.

Food has cultural significance. A recipe can remind someone of his/her parent or grandparent, country or community. Other people may find themselves limited in dietary choices by income, availability of products, health issues, working schedule or simply the tastes of their children. It is possible for an argument to be factually correct but remain unconvincing due to ignoring the people the argument aims to persuade.

It also creates a danger of forming a false dichotomy: either you are a vegetarian who cares about the environment, or you are a meat-eater who does not. But these are not the only options. If you currently eat meat every day and decide to eat it twice a week, you will considerably change your consumption. If you choose plant-based meals, but still eat some meat, you will not have "failed". The environmental aspect is relevant here according to Scarborough et al. (2023): the researchers have found not only differences between vegans and meat-eaters, but also varying levels of environmental impact according to the amount of animal-based food consumed. Thus, the reduction, not the elimination, matters.

How about nutrition? This is probably the strongest challenge to an absolute vegetarian argument. The NHS emphasizes that vegetarians need to pay close attention to iron, vitamin B12 and omega-3 fatty acids, and it also notes that some nutrients in plant foods are not absorbed and utilized by the body in exactly the same way as in animal foods (NHS, n.d.). But these concerns should not be dismissed as vegetarian myths. The acknowledgement of these concerns does not prove that vegetarian diets are nutritionally inadequate. As the British Dietetic Association notes, varied plant-based diets including beans, nuts, seeds, fruits, vegetables and whole grains can provide the necessary nutrients for good health, with an emphasis on B12 and other nutrients that require special attention in reduced/eliminated animal foods diets (British Dietetic Association, n.d.). In other words, the reasonable conclusion is not "vegetarian diets are automatically healthier." It is "well-planned vegetarian diets can be healthy, just as poorly planned omnivorous ones cannot be."

Perhaps we are asking the wrong question. Should everyone become a vegetarian? It is a good question because it gives an opportunity to form two clear sides.But I am no longer sure it is the right question.Another question would be better: Could most of us reduce meat consumption? The data give us good reasons to answer "yes". Apart from potential cardiovascular benefits of the proper vegetarian dietary patterns (Dybvik et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023), UK research on diets provides a powerful environmental argument for reduced animal-based food consumption (Scarborough et al., 2023). Meanwhile, nutrition remains relevant. Culture remains relevant. Affordability and accessibility remain relevant. Personal choice remains relevant.

Critical thinking does not require choosing the louder side of a debate. Sometimes, its role consists in changing the boundaries of the debate. So I am not arguing that everybody should wake up tomorrow, throw away meat in the fridge and announce himself/herself as a vegetarian. I am arguing for something less dramatic and potentially much more achievable: eat plants more often. Eat more beans, lentils, vegetables, nuts and whole grains. Reduce meat consumption if you currently eat too much of it. And if you chose to become a vegetarian completely, plan your nutrition properly rather than assume that "meat-free" equals "healthy".

Perhaps our diets do not need yet another identity battle. They need better questions, better evidence and less all-or-nothing thinking.

Methods

To create this argument, I have used several critical thinking methods to avoid treating vegetarianism/omnivorism as a binary debate. According to Halpern's approach to critical thinking, I have analyzed the available evidence, questioned the underlying assumptions, considered alternative explanations and evaluated the strength of conclusions rather than selecting evidence according to my initial position (Halpern, 2014). I have also used the Toulmin model of argumentation and created a qualified claim that reduced meat consumption and plant-based diet are beneficial and supported this with evidence from systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials and environmental research. Then, I have analyzed the connection between the evidence and the claim in the form of warrants, rebuttals and qualifiers, to prevent my argument from becoming an absolute one and to distinguish the well-planned vegetarian diet from just meat-free diet. I have also considered potential cognitive biases and reasoning fallacies. First, I have rejected the assumption of false dichotomy in terms of the need to become vegetarian or to eat any meat by challenging this assumption. At the same time, I have avoided overgeneralization and have distinguished the well-planned vegetarian diet from any other diet without meat. Lastly, I have protected myself from confirmation bias by intentionally including credible data about limitations and objections to vegetarian diets. This allowed me to arrive at a qualified, evidence-based conclusion.
