The Problem Isn’t Meat. It’s How Much of It We Eat.

Ask a group of people whether vegetarianism is better than eating meat and you might expect a discussion about nutrition. Instead, you could get an argument about ethics, family traditions, climate change, gym goals, money or even someone’s grandmother’s Sunday roast. Food is rarely just food.

That makes vegetarianism versus omnivorism an interesting test of critical thinking. It is tempting to pick a side and collect evidence that agrees with us. But if the question is not “Which diet can I defend?” but “What conclusion is best supported by the evidence?”, the answer becomes more complicated.

My position is therefore a hybrid one: for many people, eating predominantly plant-based foods and reducing meat consumption is a stronger choice for health and environmental sustainability. However, that does not mean everyone must become vegetarian, nor does it mean every vegetarian diet is automatically healthier or more sustainable.

So, rather than asking whether we should all become vegetarian, perhaps the better question is: how much meat do we actually need?

Health: plant-based does not automatically mean healthy

There is good reason to take plant-based eating seriously. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that vegetarian and vegan diets were associated with several favourable health outcomes, including lower risks of ischaemic heart disease and some cancers (Dinu et al., 2017). More recently, Wang et al. (2023) reviewed 76 publications involving more than two million participants and found that greater adherence to plant-based dietary patterns was associated with lower risks of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer and mortality.

That sounds like a clear win for vegetarianism. But there is an important catch: “plant-based” describes what a diet emphasises, not whether every food in it is nutritious. Chips, sugary cereals and highly processed meat substitutes can all fit into a vegetarian diet. Wang et al. (2023) found that the benefits were stronger when plant-based diets emphasised healthier foods, while less healthy plant-based patterns could be associated with poorer outcomes.

This distinction matters because it prevents us from turning a population level finding into a simplistic personal rule. A study can show that one dietary pattern is associated with better average outcomes without proving that changing diets will produce exactly the same result for every individual. Diet is only one part of health, alongside genetics, activity, sleep, smoking, alcohol and socioeconomic circumstances.

This is where the argument needs a qualifier. A well-planned and executed vegetarian diet can be nutritionally adequate, but it requires attention to nutrients such as vitamin B12, iron, calcium, iodine and omega-3 fatty acids. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics states that appropriately planned vegetarian and vegan diets can be healthful and nutritionally adequate (Melina et al., 2016).

Meanwhile, being an omnivore is not inherently unhealthy either. Meat can provide protein and important micronutrients. The issue is not simply whether meat appears on your plate; it is the overall quality and balance of the diet.

Environment: this is where eating less meat becomes harder to ignore

If health alone does not settle the argument, the environmental evidence makes reducing meat consumption harder to dismiss.

A large UK study of 55,504 people compared the environmental impacts of vegans, vegetarians, fish and meat eaters. Across greenhouse gas emissions, land use, water use, eutrophication and biodiversity, greater consumption of animal-based foods was associated with greater environmental impact (Scarborough et al., 2023). The estimated greenhouse gas footprint of vegan diets was around a quarter of that of high-meat diets.

That does not mean every plant food is environmentally perfect. Where food is produced, how it is produced and what is replacing the meat all matter. Nevertheless, the overall pattern is difficult to ignore: reducing animal focused food consumption can substantially reduce dietary environmental impacts.

The sensible conclusion, then, is not “vegetarianism is perfect”. It is that there is a strong case for eating less meat. Importantly, this does not require pretending that all animal products have identical environmental effects. Beef and lamb generally create very different impacts from some other animal foods, just as lentils, nuts and highly processed alternatives differ from one another. A useful recommendation therefore needs to focus on overall dietary patterns rather than treating every food as equally good or bad.

But what about culture, money and real life?

Here is where an argument for universal vegetarianism can become unrealistic.

Food is connected to culture, family and identity. For some people, meat is part of traditional meals, religious or cultural practices, social occasions and family routines. Others may live somewhere where particular plant foods are less accessible or affordable. A diet that looks simple and inexpensive in one household may be much harder to maintain in another.

Affordability also matters. It would be unfair to frame dietary choice entirely as an individual moral decision when people face different food prices, time pressures, cooking facilities and access to shops. For some households, the realistic change may be swapping a few meat-based meals for beans, lentils or vegetables rather than purchasing specialist vegetarian products. This makes the idea of eating less meat a more flexible recommendation than strictly saying become vegetarian. Research examining healthy, sustainable and affordable diets suggests these goals need to be considered together rather than treated as separate issues (Webb et al., 2023).

There is also the practical question of nutrition. Someone who simply removes meat without replacing its nutritional contribution may create problems for themselves. This is particularly important when dietary restrictions overlap, because removing several food groups can make adequate intake more difficult. The answer is not to dismiss vegetarianism, but to recognise that successful dietary change involves planning rather than simply subtraction.

This does not defeat the environmental or health argument. It changes the claim. Instead of saying everyone should be vegetarian, a more defensible claim is many people could benefit from reducing their reliance on meat and increasing their intake of nutritious plant foods.

And this is where critical thinking matters

Diet debates are full of reasoning traps.

Confirmation bias is one obvious example. If I already believe meat is unhealthy, I may enthusiastically share a study supporting that belief while ignoring evidence that complicates it. Conversely, if I love steak, I may focus on stories about the nutritional benefits of meat and dismiss environmental evidence. Halpern and Dunn (2023) emphasise the importance of evaluating evidence rather than allowing our existing beliefs to determine what we accept.

There is also a false dichotomy: vegetarian or omnivore, as though these are the only two meaningful positions. In reality, dietary patterns exist on a spectrum. Someone can eat meat every day, occasionally, or not at all. Treating the debate as two opposing camps makes a nuanced conclusion unnecessarily difficult.

Another problem is confusing correlation with causation. If vegetarians have lower rates of a particular disease, that does not automatically prove that avoiding meat caused the difference. Vegetarians and meat-eaters may differ in physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, socioeconomic circumstances and other health behaviours. Chapter 6 of Halpern and Dunn (2023) highlights the importance of considering alternative explanations and confounding variables when evaluating evidence.

We should also ask whether the evidence actually supports the strength of the conclusion being made. The evidence may justify reducing meat is beneficial for many people without justifying everyone must stop eating meat. In Toulmin’s terms, the evidence provides grounds for a claim, but qualifiers and rebuttals matter too.

So, what should we actually do?

For me, the strongest answer is neither eat whatever you want nor everyone must become vegetarian.

Eat more vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts and seeds. Reduce how often you eat meat, particularly when an equally satisfying plant-based alternative is available. If you choose to remain an omnivore, that does not make you irrational or uncaring. If you choose vegetarianism, that does not automatically make your diet healthier.

The goal should be a dietary pattern that is nutritionally adequate, realistic, culturally appropriate and environmentally responsible. It should also be sustainable in the everyday sense: a recommendation people can maintain rather than follow perfectly for two weeks before returning to their previous habits.

That may mean different things for different people. It also means resisting the urge to judge dietary choices using a single measure. Health, environmental impact, cost, culture and personal circumstances can all be relevant, and a strong argument should explain how these competing considerations are weighed rather than ignoring inconvenient evidence before reaching a final conclusion.

Someone who currently eats meat twice a day could make a meaningful change by reducing that to a few meals a week. Someone already eating very little meat may gain more by improving the nutritional quality of their plant foods.

The strongest argument is therefore not that vegetarianism has necessarily won. The stronger conclusion is that, on balance, reducing reliance on animal products has substantial potential benefits, while individual circumstances must be taken seriously.

Perhaps the most rational plate is not completely meat-free. Perhaps it simply has less meat on it.

Methods

This article applied critical-thinking principles from Halpern and Dunn (2023), particularly argument analysis and hypothesis testing approaches. The central argument was structured by identifying a conclusion, supporting information, assumptions, qualifiers and counterarguments. The article used a Toulmin-style approach by distinguishing the main claim from the evidence supporting it, while using qualifiers such as “for many people” to avoid overstating the evidence (Toulmin, 1958). The analysis also considered the acceptability, relevance and strength of premises rather than treating claims as equally credible. Cognitive bias was addressed through confirmation bias and the tendency to selectively favour evidence consistent with existing dietary beliefs. Halpern’s Chapter 6 concepts were applied by distinguishing association from causation and considering confounding variables, such as differences in physical activity, smoking and other health behaviours between dietary groups. Alternative explanations and individual variability were also considered when interpreting health evidence. Finally, the article deliberately challenged both views of solely vegetarianism and omnivorism by presenting reduced meat consumption as a third, more qualified position.
