AI Belongs in the Classroom - But It Should Never Replace Thinking Artificial intelligence has arrived in education whether schools, universities and governments are ready for it or not. Students can now ask an AI chatbot to explain a difficult concept, summarise a chapter or provide feedback on their writing within seconds. Teachers can use AI to create lesson materials, identify students who may need additional support and personalise learning activities. The question is no longer whether artificial intelligence will enter the classroom. It already has. The more important question is whether education will use it wisely. I believe AI should be integrated into education, but not as a replacement for teachers or independent thinking. Used properly, it can make learning more accessible, personalised and effective. Used carelessly, it risks weakening the very skills education is supposed to develop. The strongest position is therefore neither unconditional enthusiasm nor complete rejection. AI should support human education, not replace it. The argument for bringing AI into education One of the strongest arguments for AI in education is its potential to personalise learning. Traditional classrooms often require one teacher to support dozens of students with different abilities, interests and learning speeds. This makes individual attention difficult. An AI-powered learning system, however, can provide immediate feedback and adapt activities according to a student's performance. For example, a student struggling with mathematics could receive additional explanations and practice questions targeted at their specific weaknesses. Another student who has already mastered the material could progress to more challenging content. A systematic review of AI-based personalised learning research found that artificial intelligence can support adaptive learning pathways, improve engagement and provide tailored feedback (Farhood et al., 2025). This does not mean that AI automatically makes education better. Personalisation only has educational value when it actually improves understanding. Nevertheless, the possibility of providing students with support whenever they need it is significant. Human teachers are essential, but they cannot realistically provide unlimited one-to-one tutoring to every learner. Recent experimental research also provides encouraging evidence. Kestin et al. (2025) conducted a randomised controlled trial comparing an AI-powered tutor with active learning in a university physics course. Students using the AI tutor demonstrated greater learning gains in less time and reported higher levels of engagement and motivation. These findings suggest that AI can do more than simply automate administrative tasks: under carefully designed conditions, it may directly improve learning outcomes. AI can also improve accessibility. Students with disabilities, language difficulties or limited access to private tutoring may benefit from tools that provide explanations in different formats and at different speeds. Text-to-speech systems, translation tools and conversational assistants can help remove barriers that have historically prevented some students from fully participating in education. There is also an argument about efficiency. Teachers spend considerable time preparing materials, marking work and completing administrative tasks. AI could reduce some of this workload, allowing educators to spend more time on activities that require uniquely human skills, such as mentoring, emotional support, discussion and developing students' confidence. However, these benefits should not be confused with evidence that AI is universally beneficial. The technology itself is not the intervention. How it is designed and used matters considerably. The risks of allowing AI to do too much The most obvious concern is academic dishonesty. Generative AI can produce essays, solve problems and answer questions in seconds. If students submit AI-generated work as their own, they may receive qualifications without developing the knowledge and skills those qualifications are intended to represent. This problem is not simply about cheating. It raises a deeper educational question: what is the purpose of assessment? If the purpose of an essay is to demonstrate independent reasoning, then outsourcing the reasoning to an AI system undermines the assessment. A systematic review of research on AI and academic integrity concluded that artificial intelligence creates both opportunities and challenges, with responsible use requiring stronger ethical guidance and a culture of academic honesty (Al-Darei et al., 2025). There is also a danger that students become dependent on AI. Learning often requires struggling with difficult information, making mistakes and gradually developing the ability to solve problems independently. If AI provides an instant answer every time something becomes challenging, students may avoid the productive effort required for genuine understanding. This concern is particularly important because convenience can easily be mistaken for learning. A student who receives a correct answer may feel successful without actually understanding why the answer is correct. The issue can be explained through critical thinking. Good reasoning requires individuals to evaluate information, consider alternatives and reach conclusions based on evidence. If students simply accept AI-generated answers without questioning them, they may become less active participants in their own education. AI systems are also not infallible. They can generate inaccurate information, reflect biases in their training data and present confident answers that are unsupported. Students who assume that technology is objective may fail to question its output. This creates another problem: cognitive bias. Humans are vulnerable to automation bias, the tendency to trust recommendations from automated systems. When an AI produces an answer fluently and confidently, users may assume that it must be correct. In education, this could encourage students to accept information without independently verifying it. Privacy is another serious concern. Personalised learning systems often require large amounts of student data, including performance information, behavioural patterns and sometimes personal characteristics. UNESCO (2023) has warned that generative AI cannot be integrated responsibly without safeguards relating to privacy, equity, regulation and human oversight. These concerns are not arguments against all educational technology. They are arguments against careless implementation. Looking beyond the false choice between humans and machines Much of the debate surrounding AI in education is unnecessarily polarised. Supporters sometimes present AI as the solution to every educational problem, while critics portray it as a threat that will destroy traditional learning. Both positions contain assumptions that deserve examination. The assumption that technology automatically improves education is questionable. Previous innovations, from educational television to online learning platforms, demonstrated that technological availability does not guarantee educational effectiveness. Learning depends on pedagogy, motivation, feedback and the quality of interaction between teachers and students. Equally, the assumption that AI necessarily destroys independent thinking is also too simplistic. A calculator does not prevent someone from understanding mathematics, and a dictionary does not prevent someone from learning vocabulary. The effect depends on whether the tool supports understanding or replaces the mental activity required to develop it. This distinction is central to evaluating the evidence. AI can be used to provide an answer, but it can also be used to encourage a student to discover an answer independently. For example, instead of asking AI to write an essay, a student could use it to identify weaknesses in their argument, generate opposing viewpoints or explain a difficult theory. These uses require students to remain cognitively engaged. The strongest evidence therefore supports a conditional conclusion rather than an absolute one. AI appears capable of improving learning under structured and pedagogically informed conditions, but its effectiveness depends on implementation. The recent research on AI tutoring is promising, but one successful randomised controlled trial cannot establish that every AI system will improve education. Differences in age, subject, institution and educational context must also be considered. This is why critical thinking matters. Evidence should inform decisions, but evidence must also be evaluated. A persuasive argument cannot simply collect studies that support one side while ignoring contradictory findings. What responsible AI integration should look like Rather than banning AI or allowing unrestricted use, schools and universities should establish clear principles for responsible integration. First, AI should supplement teachers rather than replace them. Education involves more than transferring information. Teachers provide encouragement, judgement, social interaction and emotional support. These human relationships cannot be fully replicated by algorithms. Second, students should be explicitly taught how to evaluate AI-generated information. AI literacy should become part of education itself. Students need to understand that AI can be useful but can also be inaccurate, biased and limited. Learning how to question technology is just as important as learning how to use it. Third, assessment methods should evolve. If assignments can easily be generated by AI, education institutions need to place greater emphasis on processes that demonstrate genuine understanding. This could include oral presentations, classroom discussions, reflective writing, supervised assessments and coursework that requires students to explain and defend their reasoning. Finally, privacy and equality must be prioritised. Students should not be required to surrender excessive personal data simply to access educational opportunities. Institutions must also ensure that AI does not widen inequalities between students who have access to advanced technology and those who do not. UNESCO's (2023) guidance reflects this human-centred approach, emphasising ethical, safe and equitable use rather than technological adoption for its own sake. The future of education should be human-centred Artificial intelligence is neither the saviour nor the enemy of education. It is a powerful tool whose consequences will depend largely on the decisions made by educators, institutions and policymakers. Rejecting AI completely would mean ignoring genuine opportunities to improve accessibility, personalise learning and support teachers. Allowing AI to replace independent thought would be equally irresponsible. The goal should not be to create students who can produce answers faster than machines. Machines are already exceptionally good at that. The goal should be to create students who can ask better questions, evaluate evidence, recognise uncertainty and think independently. AI belongs in the classroom, but it should remain a tool rather than the teacher. Education must continue to develop human judgement, curiosity and critical thinking. If technology supports those abilities rather than replacing them, AI could become one of the most valuable educational innovations of our time. The future of education should not be about humans versus machines. It should be about using machines wisely enough to help humans learn better. Methods Several critical thinking concepts and argumentation techniques were applied in developing this blog article. Toulmin's Model of Argumentation was used to structure the central argument through a clear claim, supporting evidence, warrants, qualifiers and rebuttals (Toulmin, 2003). The article presents the claim that AI should be integrated into education under human oversight, supported by empirical research and policy guidance. Critical analysis was applied by evaluating both the benefits and limitations of AI, rather than accepting evidence uncritically. Alternative viewpoints were considered to avoid confirmation bias, particularly the assumption that AI is either entirely beneficial or entirely harmful. The argument also applies principles of evidence-based reasoning by distinguishing between promising findings and conclusions that can be generalised across all educational settings. Finally, the article recognises potential automation bias, whereby individuals may place excessive trust in automated systems.
