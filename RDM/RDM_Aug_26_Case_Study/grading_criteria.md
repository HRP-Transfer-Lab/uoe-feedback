# RDM Case Study Blog — Subcriterion Scoring Rubric

## 1. Purpose of the assessment

Treat this as a critical-thinking assignment expressed through one of three substantive topics:

- Vegetarianism vs omnivorism
- Integration of AI in education
- Online vs traditional education

The topic is the vehicle for demonstrating critical thinking. Do not treat strong subject content alone as equivalent to strong critical analysis.

The student should construct a persuasive, evidence-based HuffPost-style argument and then explain in one Methods paragraph which critical-thinking, reasoning, argumentation or persuasion methods were used.

Assess whether the student:

- formulates a clear argument;
- evaluates evidence rather than merely reporting it;
- examines assumptions and alternative explanations;
- evaluates inferential strength and uncertainty;
- considers counterarguments;
- identifies or controls biases and fallacies;
- applies relevant critical-thinking/argumentation theory;
- persuades ethically and appropriately for a public audience.

## 2. Internal subcriterion scoring

Score every subcriterion using only:

**1 = Fully demonstrated**  
Clearly, accurately and consistently fulfilled, with appropriate understanding, application or integration.

**0.5 = Partially demonstrated**  
Present but incomplete, inconsistent, underdeveloped or containing conceptual limitations.

**0 = Not demonstrated**  
Absent, seriously inaccurate, unsupported or not identifiable.

A concept should not receive 1 merely because it is named. The student must demonstrate understanding or application.

At this scoring stage, do **not** convert these point scores into criterion percentage marks or an overall assignment grade.

## 3. Criterion 1: Knowledge and Understanding

### A. Critical-thinking and argumentation methods
Evaluate whether the Methods paragraph identifies and explains relevant critical-thinking, reasoning or argumentation methods rather than merely listing them.

- **1:** Relevant methods are accurately explained and selected purposefully.
- **0.5:** Methods are named but explanation or application is partial.
- **0:** No identifiable method, or serious misunderstanding.

### B. Core module reading
Evaluate engagement with the designated Halpern and Dunn core textbook.

- **1:** Used accurately to explain or apply a central concept or method.
- **0.5:** Cited but only superficially applied.
- **0:** Not used, or another Halpern source substitutes for the designated core textbook.

### C. Anatomy of the argument
Evaluate understanding of the components relevant to the student's argument, including:

- principal claim or conclusion;
- premises, reasons or evidence;
- assumptions or warrants;
- qualifiers;
- counterarguments;
- rebuttals or responses.

A score of 1 requires accurate understanding of the main components relevant to the argument, not merely use of the terminology.

### D. Reasoning and inference
Evaluate whether the student identifies the principal form of reasoning as inductive, deductive, causal, probabilistic or a justified combination and explains the level of certainty that reasoning permits.

- **1:** Both the form of reasoning and its implications for certainty are accurately explained.
- **0.5:** Reasoning is identified but certainty is not explained, or the explanation is incomplete.
- **0:** The form of reasoning is absent or seriously misunderstood.

### E. Definitions and conceptual accuracy
Evaluate whether important critical-thinking and topic-specific concepts are accurately defined where a general reader would need clarification. Definitions should be concise and integrated into the discussion.

## 4. Criterion 2: Critical Analysis and Evaluation

This criterion must emphasise **meta-epistemic critical thinking**, not merely balanced topic coverage. The student should evaluate not only *what* different sources say, but *why* particular claims should be believed, how strongly the evidence supports them and what limits the resulting conclusions.

### A. Coherent argument architecture
Evaluate whether there is a clear central position and a coherent progression from reasons and evidence towards the conclusion. The relationship between claims, evidence, assumptions and conclusions should be understandable.

### B. Quality and relevance of evidence
Evaluate whether important factual and causal claims are supported by relevant, credible evidence and whether the student distinguishes stronger evidence from weaker evidence rather than treating all sources as equivalent.

### C. Evaluation of inferential strength
Evaluate whether the student asks what the evidence actually establishes. Look for appropriate consideration of:

- correlation versus causation;
- necessary versus probable conclusions;
- strength of causal claims;
- overgeneralisation;
- alternative explanations;
- whether conclusions exceed the evidence.

### D. Assumptions, limitations and uncertainty
Evaluate whether the student explicitly examines relevant:

- hidden assumptions;
- methodological limitations;
- population or context limitations;
- uncertainty;
- generalisability;
- boundary conditions.

### E. Counterarguments and alternative perspectives
Evaluate whether significant opposing positions are represented fairly and in sufficient detail, and whether they are answered using evidence and reasoning rather than dismissed. Appropriate concessions or modification of the original position should be recognised positively.

### F. Bias and fallacy control
Evaluate relevant awareness, avoidance or correction of biases and fallacies such as:

- confirmation bias;
- myside bias;
- belief bias;
- framing effects;
- availability bias;
- false dichotomy;
- overgeneralisation;
- appeal to novelty or tradition;
- straw person reasoning;
- appeal to anecdote or ignorance.

Merely presenting a list of pros and cons is not sufficient for full credit. High-quality performance requires evaluation of the evidential and inferential basis of claims.

## 5. Criterion 3: Application of Theory to Persuasive Blog Writing

### A. Application of an argumentation method
Evaluate whether an evidence-based argumentation framework, such as Toulmin, visibly shapes the actual blog argument rather than appearing only in the Methods paragraph. The Methods paragraph should explicitly use the term argument or argumentation and explain how the framework was applied.

### B. Application of persuasion techniques
Evaluate appropriate evidence-based persuasion techniques, for example:

- establishing source credibility;
- two-sided messaging;
- anticipating counterarguments;
- avoiding an obviously one-sided presentation;
- presenting a clear conclusion;
- using multiple supporting reasons;
- narrative plus evidence;
- concrete examples or analogies;
- making the argument easy to understand.

The Methods paragraph should identify and explain at least some of the persuasion techniques actually used.

### C. Ethical and evidence-based persuasion
Evaluate whether the student persuades through evidence, reasoning and fair representation rather than exaggeration, emotional manipulation or selective omission. Personal or emotional material may support the argument but should not replace evidence.

### D. Audience and genre adaptation
Evaluate whether the submission reads as an accessible public-facing HuffPost-style blog rather than a conventional academic essay or literature review. Relevant features include an engaging and descriptive title, effective opening, informative subheadings, recognisable authorial voice and direct reader engagement.

### E. Gricean communication
Evaluate whether the blog is sufficiently informative, relevant, clear and orderly, and whether evidence is communicated without misleading implication. If Grice is claimed as a method, it should be identified explicitly in the Methods paragraph.

## 6. Criterion 4: Presentation and Academic Conventions

### A. Clarity and concision
Sentences and paragraphs should be clear, readable and appropriately concise, avoiding unnecessary jargon, repetition and vague claims.

### B. Organisation and progression
Paragraphs, sections, headings and transitions should contribute to the development of the central argument rather than forming disconnected source summaries.

### C. Introduction and conclusion
The introduction should establish the issue, significance and central position or question. The conclusion should synthesise the reasoning, answer the central question and communicate an appropriately qualified judgement without simply repeating the introduction or adding substantial new evidence.

### D. Concrete communication
Abstract ideas should be clarified through relevant examples, scenarios, comparisons, analogies or real-life applications. Examples should illustrate the argument rather than function as sufficient evidence by themselves.

### E. Accuracy of written English
Grammar, punctuation and spelling should be accurate, with consistent British English. Distinguish minor errors from frequent errors that interfere with clarity.

### F. APA 7 formatting
Evaluate the accuracy and consistency of in-text citations and the References section, including author-date citation, alphabetical ordering, italics where required and DOI presentation.

### G. Required structure and word limit
The required order is:

**Blog Post → Methods → References**

The blog should be **1,350–1,650 words**. The Methods paragraph and References section are excluded from this word limit. The Methods section should be clearly labelled and presented as **one paragraph**.

- **1:** Required order, word limit and one-paragraph Methods requirement are fulfilled.
- **0.5:** Minor deviation.
- **0:** Major structural omission or substantial word-limit problem.

## 7. Criterion 5: Reading and Referencing

### A. Range and relevance of reading
Evaluate whether the References section contains a sufficient range of substantive and relevant sources.

- **1:** At least 10 substantive, relevant sources.
- **0.5:** 6–9 substantive sources, or 10+ sources with substantial relevance problems.
- **0:** 5 or fewer substantive sources.

### B. Academic quality of sources
The majority of sources should be credible academic materials such as peer-reviewed journal articles, systematic reviews or meta-analyses, recognised academic textbooks, classic theoretical works or authoritative institutional reports where appropriate.

### C. Balance of recent and foundational research
Evaluate whether the reading combines sufficiently recent topic research with older foundational sources where theoretically important. Recent publication alone should not be treated as evidence of quality.

### D. Use of primary and secondary evidence
Evaluate whether important empirical claims are traced to primary research where reasonably possible rather than relying mainly on textbooks, popular summaries, websites or secondary accounts.

### E. Use of evidence synthesis
Evaluate whether the student uses at least one relevant systematic review, meta-analysis or high-quality scholarly review where suitable evidence synthesis is available.

### F. Integration of sources
Evaluate whether sources are compared, evaluated and integrated into the argument rather than simply presented one after another or used decoratively. The relevance, strength or limitations of important evidence should be explained.

Do not penalise a student simply because most references concern the chosen content topic. The substantive topic requires an appropriate evidence base. The issue is whether the submission also demonstrates sufficient engagement with the critical-thinking, argumentation and persuasion literature used to construct the assignment.

## Later stages: conversion and student feedback

The 0 / 0.5 / 1 instructions above govern Stage 1. After cross-cohort calibration, follow [the version 2 grading method](GRADING_METHOD_Independent_Subcriteria.md): normalise raw criterion totals, apply university weights, derive the common cohort conversion and apply it to every criterion, then calculate the weighted processed overall and mean-preserving whole-number rounding. Do not introduce independent percentage judgements or a separate overall-only uplift. Use [FEEDBACK_INSTRUCTIONS.md](FEEDBACK_INSTRUCTIONS.md) for narrative feedback. Existing version 1 percentage outputs and the old example are historical until regenerated.
