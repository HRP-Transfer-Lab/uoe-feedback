AI Belongs in Education — But It Should Never Do the Thinking for Us

Artificial intelligence is no longer something we only see in science-fiction films. It is already sitting in our classrooms, helping students write essays, find information, explain difficult topics and even generate ideas in seconds. For students, this can feel like having a personal tutor available 24/7. But there is a problem we cannot ignore: if AI starts doing too much of the thinking for us, are we actually learning? AI has the potential to make education more accessible, personalised and effective, but it should support students’ thinking rather than replace it. The future of education should not be about choosing between humans and AI; it should be about learning how to use AI without losing the ability to think for ourselves.

How AI can make learning better

Used in the right way, AI can make learning more flexible and personalised. Students do not all learn at the same pace or in the same way, and traditional teaching cannot always provide individual support whenever it is needed. AI tools can help by explaining difficult concepts in simpler language, providing immediate feedback and offering different ways of approaching a problem. Research suggests that AI-supported learning can provide personalised feedback and support students’ learning processes, potentially helping them understand difficult material more effectively (Wang et al., 2026). AI can also encourage students to become more active learners when it is used as a starting point rather than an ending point. For example, instead of asking AI to write an entire essay, a student could ask it to explain a difficult concept, suggest questions to investigate or provide an alternative perspective. The student can then compare those suggestions with lecture materials, textbooks and academic research. In this situation, AI does not replace the learning process; it becomes part of it. Recent research suggests that the educational benefits of generative AI depend heavily on how it is used. Studies have found positive effects on higher-order thinking and critical thinking when AI use is structured and supported by activities that require students to evaluate, reflect and reason (Zhao et al., 2025). This suggests that the question should not simply be whether students use AI, but whether they are using it in a way that keeps them intellectually engaged.

When convenience becomes a problem

However, the same technology that makes learning easier can also make it easier for students to avoid learning altogether. When an answer can be generated within seconds, there is a temptation to accept it without questioning how or why the answer was produced. The biggest problem, therefore, may not be AI itself but passive dependence on it. There is an important difference between asking AI to challenge your thinking and asking it to think for you. A student who asks AI to provide several possible explanations and then evaluates them is engaging with the learning process. A student who copies the first response without checking it is doing something very different. This distinction matters because AI-generated content can appear confident and authoritative even when it contains errors or lacks important context. Recent research has highlighted both sides of this issue: generative AI can support critical thinking when students are given structured opportunities to question and evaluate its output, but unguided use can encourage cognitive offloading and reduce analytical engagement (Suazo Galdames et al., 2026).

Keep critical thinking at the centre

This is where critical thinking becomes essential. Critical thinking involves more than simply knowing information; it requires us to question claims, consider evidence, recognise assumptions and reach reasoned conclusions. When students use AI, they need to apply these skills rather than automatically trusting what it produces. AI can generate information that sounds convincing even when it is incomplete, inaccurate or presented without enough context. A student who simply copies an AI-generated answer may never notice these problems. In contrast, a critical thinker would ask: Where did this information come from? Is there evidence to support it? Could there be another explanation? By asking these questions, students remain actively involved in the learning process. AI can provide an answer, but it cannot replace the human responsibility to decide whether that answer is accurate, relevant and worth accepting. Critical thinking is particularly important because AI-generated answers can present an argument without making its weaknesses obvious. Students therefore need to look beyond the conclusion and consider the reasons behind it. Halpern and Dunn describe argument analysis as a process of examining how reasons and evidence support a conclusion, while also considering assumptions and counterarguments. This provides a useful way for students to approach AI-generated information. Instead of asking only whether an AI response sounds convincing, they can ask what evidence supports its claims, whether important assumptions have been made and whether alternative explanations have been ignored. This approach changes AI from something that simply provides answers into something that students can critically question. In this sense, using AI effectively requires students to remain active thinkers rather than passive receivers of information.

AI should support teachers, not replace them

There is also a wider question about what happens to education when AI becomes part of everyday academic work. Teachers are not simply there to provide students with information; they help students develop skills, challenge their ideas and recognise where their reasoning needs improvement. AI cannot fully replace this human interaction. A teacher can understand a student’s difficulties, ask follow-up questions and encourage them to think more deeply about an issue. AI can provide immediate feedback, but students still need human guidance to understand when that feedback is useful and when it should be questioned.

Using AI without losing academic integrity

There is also the issue of academic integrity. If a student submits AI-generated work as their own, they may receive a grade without demonstrating the skills that the assessment was designed to measure. This creates a problem not only for universities but also for the student themselves. A qualification is valuable because it represents knowledge and skills that a person has developed, not simply their ability to produce a polished piece of writing. Using AI transparently and within academic rules allows students to benefit from the technology without misrepresenting their own work. The aim should therefore be to develop responsible AI users who can explain, evaluate and defend their own ideas.

Ultimately, education needs to adapt rather than pretend that AI does not exist. Students are already using these technologies, and attempting to ban them completely may not teach students how to use them responsibly. Instead, universities and teachers can focus on teaching students how to question AI outputs, verify information, acknowledge when AI has been used and combine AI assistance with their own reasoning. This approach recognises the reality of modern education while protecting the skills that education is supposed to develop. AI may change how students learn, but it should not change the fundamental purpose of learning: developing knowledgeable, independent and critical thinkers.

So where should AI fit in education?

AI is not the enemy of education. In fact, when used responsibly, it can be a powerful tool that helps students understand difficult ideas, receive feedback and learn in ways that suit them. The real danger begins when convenience replaces curiosity and AI starts doing the thinking that students should be doing themselves. Education should prepare students not only to find answers, but to question them, evaluate evidence and make informed decisions. This is why students should not be encouraged to avoid AI, but to learn how to use it critically and responsibly. The goal should be simple: let AI support the learning process, while keeping human thinking at the centre of it. After all, the most valuable skill education can give us is not the ability to produce an answer quickly, but the ability to think about whether that answer is actually worth believing

.

Methods

This blog article was developed using principles of critical thinking outlined by Halpern and Dunn (2023), particularly argument analysis, evaluation of evidence and reasoned judgement. Argument analysis was used to examine the different claims surrounding the use of artificial intelligence in education, including both its potential benefits and possible disadvantages. Rather than presenting AI as either entirely positive or negative, the article considers different perspectives and evaluates the reasoning behind them. Evaluation of evidence was also important because AI can provide information that appears convincing but may not always be accurate or sufficiently supported. The blog therefore emphasises the importance of questioning information, checking evidence and considering whether conclusions are justified. In addition, the concept of reasoned judgement was applied when developing the overall argument that AI should support, rather than replace, students’ thinking. This involved considering the evidence for the educational benefits of AI alongside concerns about dependence, academic integrity and reduced engagement with learning. These critical-thinking principles helped ensure that the argument was balanced, evidence-based and based on reasoned evaluation rather than simply personal opinion.
