Is Online Education as Good as the Classroom? Maybe We Are Asking the Wrong Question

The COVID-19 pandemic transformed education, forcing universities to move rapidly from traditional classrooms to online platforms. Years later, although universities have largely returned to in-person teaching, the shift has changed how higher education is delivered. This has sparked an important debate: is online education as effective as in-person learning?

Students who have experienced both traditional university education and online learning may have differing opinions, making it tempting for them to choose a "winner". However, we should first question the debate's underlying assumption: "If face-to-face is better, should we not expect students in classrooms to achieve better learning outcomes compared to online learning consistently?"

If Face-to-Face is Better, Where is the Evidence?

A pervasive belief is that face-to-face learning is inherently better than online education. The crucial question is: how much of this is based on evidence, and how much may reflect confirmation bias—the tendency to favour information that supports our pre-existing beliefs?

Halpern and Dunn explain how confirmation bias can influence our evaluation, not just in education but in our daily lives as well. This is particularly relevant when comparing these two educational formats. If we already believe that traditional in-person learning yields better results, we might be more likely to notice evidence supporting that view while overlooking evidence to the contrary.

Research by Kemp and Grieve, as well as by Woldeab and colleagues, can help us think more critically about this assumption. Kemp and Grieve found that although many students prefer face-to-face interaction, this does not always lead to better learning outcomes. Moreover, Woldeab and colleagues found no significant difference between online and face-to-face courses.

This challenges the assumption that physical presence automatically leads to better learning. Thus, the emphasis should shift from "where" learning occurs to "how" effectively students can apply their knowledge.

So, What Does Online Learning Get Right?

If face-to-face learning is not automatically better, the next question is whether online education offers advantages that can genuinely contribute to effective learning. It does, but these advantages should not be conflated with the idea that online education is universally superior.

One of the main advantages of online education is its flexibility, which lets students balance their studies with other commitments and stay engaged and motivated. Vlasie argues that online platforms give students greater access to tailored resources, enable personalised learning paths, and foster greater autonomy. Meanwhile, Montenegro Rueda and colleagues highlight the importance of flexibility, accessibility, and digital competencies, which are crucial in our technological world.

This matters because learning effectiveness depends not only on location but also on whether the learning environment accommodates students' individual circumstances. For students managing employment, family responsibilities, or geographical limitations, flexibility is not just a convenience; it represents an opportunity that makes higher education accessible. Thus, this flexibility can be an important factor in widening access to higher education.

Evidence also suggests that online learning, particularly in synchronous formats, can support positive affective and cognitive outcomes for learners. Gegenfurtner and colleagues found that synchronous online learning can support cognitive and affective outcomes, with webinars showing positive effects on learning performance and outcomes such as self-efficacy, satisfaction and interest. However, these advantages do not establish that online learning produces better outcomes in every context. They show that online education can create conditions that support effective learning, especially when educators employ strategies that challenge students' thinking while also addressing their cognitive needs.

But What Does the Classroom Still Do Better?

However, focusing solely on the advantages of online education would be incomplete. If flexibility, accessibility, and improved cognitive outcomes support learning, we must also critically examine what physical presence in a classroom provides that online education may struggle to replicate.

One key advantage students report is social interaction. Kemp and Grieve noted that in-person learning fosters collaboration, mentorship, and discussion, creating opportunities for immediate feedback and community building. These features may contribute to engagement and comprehension in ways that cannot simply be dismissed.

Students' social and emotional experiences in online learning environments deserve serious consideration. Many students report feeling isolated, which can hinder their engagement and motivation. This isolation is not merely a personal issue; it can also affect students’ engagement and academic experiences. Akpen and colleagues highlighted several challenges associated with online learning, noting social isolation, decreased engagement, and reduced interaction with instructors and peers. Therefore, educators should prioritise social connection and interaction when designing online learning environments. Ignoring these factors can not only impact students but also undermine the overall effectiveness of educational systems.

This presents a genuine challenge to the argument for online education. If effective learning often depends on interaction, feedback, and a sense of community, then flexibility, accessibility, and improved learner outcomes alone cannot validate online education as the superior option. Instead, it emphasises that the effectiveness of educational settings—whether online or face-to-face—depends significantly on the diverse resources they provide and the unique needs of the students they serve. Understanding these nuances is crucial, as each mode of education presents its own strengths and weaknesses. Therefore, it is important to acknowledge these differences and strive to enhance both online and in-person learning experiences in ways that address the comprehensive needs of all students.

The Problem with the Comparison

At this point, the evidence challenges the idea of selecting a single winner between online and face-to-face education. While online education boasts several advantages, traditional classroom learning equally merits recognition for its strengths.

A face-to-face classroom can be highly interactive, allowing students to receive immediate feedback, but classroom learning can also become passive, leaving students disengaged. On the other hand, an online course might facilitate vibrant discussions and collaborative, intricate virtual activities, while another may rely predominantly on recorded lectures.

This distinction matters because comparing "online" with "traditional" education as though each were a single educational method risks attributing differences in outcomes solely to the learning environment. In reality, these differences often arise from course design, teaching methodologies, or the interplay between technology and pedagogy. As mentioned, research has found few to no significant differences in outcomes between online and traditional courses, suggesting that delivery mode alone cannot explain educational effectiveness. For instance, while Lassiter notes that students enrolled in in-person learning strategy courses tend to earn higher grades, this does not translate into significantly improved GPAs, persistence, or retention rates. Thus, these findings suggest advantages in specific outcomes without establishing clear overall superiority of one method over the other.

Context also plays a crucial role in this debate. Makhoba identifies undeniable benefits of online learning, such as greater independence and improved access to resources. Yet, he also cautions us to acknowledge the realities of the digital divide and the challenge of inadequate internet connectivity. A student might thrive on the flexibility of online education but still suffer setbacks because of limited access to necessary technology.

The evidence suggests that neither mode is superior. Instead, it proposes that an educational format's effectiveness depends partly on the learner, the learning goals, the course design, and the conditions under which learning occurs.

Does Combining Both Learning Methods Solve the Problem?

The debate over whether online or face-to-face education is superior continues, and hybrid learning has emerged as a potentially effective option in certain contexts. For instance, Guo and colleagues found that hybrid learning led to stronger learning outcomes in their study, although these results were specific to a particular population and context.

Hybrid approaches can harness the strengths of both online and in-person education when they are aligned with the context and learning goals. Batool and colleagues suggest that blending different teaching methods can provide potential benefits. This can be achieved by utilising tools such as discussion boards and virtual simulations, which help maintain student engagement and provide essential interactivity. Nevertheless, successful hybrid learning requires careful consideration of instructional design, learner needs, and course objectives, as it is not a one-size-fits-all solution.

Beyond the Online vs. Classroom Debate

Instead of framing the conversation around which method is better, we should interrogate whom each approach benefits and under what specific conditions. This perspective, as Wang and colleagues highlight, shifts our focus toward a more constructive examination of educational effectiveness. Ultimately, the crux of the issue lies in questioning our fundamental assumptions. Rather than asking, "Is online or face-to-face better?" we should be evaluating, "Which approach is most effective for which learners, for what educational goals, and in what contexts?" The answer may fluctuate—sometimes favouring online formats, at other times highlighting the benefits of face-to-face interaction, and occasionally revealing that a well-designed hybrid option is the favoured choice.

The goal should not be to declare a definitive winner, but to identify the learning environment that best meets learners' diverse needs and objectives.

Methods

This blog article employs a persuasive, evidence-based approach to address a critical question, present relevant information, and reach a well-substantiated conclusion. The analysis draws on critical-thinking concepts from Units 4–6 of Halpern and Dunn (2023). The logical principle of Modus Ponens (Wason, 1966) is applied to evaluate the assumption that face-to-face education is inherently superior: "If face-to-face is better, should we not expect students in classrooms to achieve better learning outcomes compared to online learning consistently?" The available evidence is then analysed to assess this assumption, separating the logical structure from the evidential strength of its premises. Furthermore, Toulmin's Model of Argument (Toulmin, 1958) is employed to organise the central claim, supporting grounds, and warrant. Qualifiers and counterarguments are incorporated to acknowledge the limitations of the evidence. For example, evidence that challenges the effectiveness of face-to-face learning is considered a counterargument rather than being dismissed. The persuasive techniques outlined by Halpern and Dunn (2023) inform the argument's development, highlighting the importance of credible evidence, multiple supporting reasons, and diverse perspectives. Ultimately, the argument is synthesised into a coherent conclusion. By using these methods, the article presents a well-reasoned, persuasive argument while remaining thorough and avoiding one-sided or overly generalised claims.
